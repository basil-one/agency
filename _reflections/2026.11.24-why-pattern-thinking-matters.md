We've all walked away from conversations feeling the weight of something familiar.

Perhaps it's the disappointment of not being understood, the frustration of finding ourselves in the same conflict, or the sadness of feeling a little further apart from someone we care about.

We may have entered the conversation with the best of intentions.

And yet, here we are again.

A recent conversation with a Zen master prompted me to reflect on the nature of patterns and what becomes possible when we learn to recognize them.

## Patterns Within Us

Through personal exploration and leadership coaching, I've found it helpful to return to a few questions when reflecting on my experiences.

How am I interpreting what's happening?

How am I engaging with it?

Am I oriented toward protecting something, remaining open, or exploring what's possible?

Over time, these questions have become [lenses](https://circle.basil.one/lenses/) through which I notice patterns in myself and others: sense making, energy awareness, and paradigm awareness.

Energy awareness has been a particularly meaningful starting point for me.

I can recognize that a conversation needs more listening and still feel the urgency to drive it toward resolution.

Knowing how I'd like to respond and being able to respond that way are different capabilities.

This is what drew me to the [Focus Energy Balance Indicator (FEBI®)](https://zenleader.global/resources/febi-assessment). It integrates mind and body by design, reflecting its [origins in the study of neuromuscular patterns](https://zenleader.global/s/FEBI-Technical-Manual-2024.pdf).

Through personal practice and coaching others, I've come to appreciate how movement, breath, and other embodied practices can expand the ways we're able to engage with the world.

## Patterns Between Us

As we become more aware of our own patterns, we can also begin to notice the patterns we participate in.

Perhaps the harder I push for resolution, the more you withdraw. And the more you withdraw, the harder I push.

Neither of us may intend for this to happen.

Yet together, we sustain something that neither of us creates alone.

Similar patterns emerge in families, teams, and communities, shaped by the expectations, constraints, and shared histories surrounding us.

Sometimes the difficulty we're experiencing isn't something within us that needs to change.

It's something between us that needs to be understood, or something around us that needs to change.

Recognizing the nature of a pattern helps us discover where we might have influence.

## Patterns That Bring Life

Noticing patterns isn't only useful when something isn't working.

We can bring the same curiosity to experiences that nourish us.

A place where we naturally want to linger. A friendship in which we feel at ease. A conversation that leaves us feeling more connected and open to possibility.

What gives these experiences their sense of life?

This is part of what draws me to the [PLoP community](https://plopcon.org/), which grew out of the pattern tradition seeded by architect Christopher Alexander and his exploration of living structures.

I appreciate the spirit of coming together to discover and share patterns drawn from lived experience.

We can learn from one another about what brings life, the conditions that support it, and what we might cultivate in our own circumstances.

## An Embodied Mindset

When I speak of *pattern thinking*, what I'm cultivating is an embodied mindset.

This is one of the reasons I practice Aikido at [Tenshinkan Dojo](https://japaneseculturecenter.com/classes/aikido).

On the mat, I can understand the principle of working with forces rather than opposing them. But when someone actually applies force, I discover how deeply I've integrated that understanding.

I encounter that same distinction in relationships, conversations, and the work of leading change.

Through practice, what we understand can gradually become part of how we meet the world.

And when I find myself in that familiar territory again, I have something to return to.

An inquiry into what's happening within me, between us, and around us.

Not only into what's sustaining the difficulty, but also into what might bring life.

**Perhaps patterns matter most when they help us recognize and cultivate what brings life.**
