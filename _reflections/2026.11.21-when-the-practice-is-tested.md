I was recently talking with an Aikido sensei about hierarchy. He described the hierarchy of a dojo as more like a wheel than a line.

I like that image.

A line suggests positions above and below one another. A wheel allows different relationships to a shared center, with some room to find our place and negotiate our distance.

But it left me wondering: **What actually holds the center when things become difficult?**

## When Human Tension Rises

I've spent time in communities organized around shared practices and intentions. Aikido, Zen, meditation, mindfulness, authentic relating, faith-based communities, open source, and communities within corporate organizations.

Their structures vary, but people gather around something they value and hope to develop through practice.

We bring our preferences, attachments, relationships, interpretations, and different capacities for navigating difficulty.

I've noticed that the distinction between principle-centered and ego-centered order becomes most visible under pressure.

When someone questions a decision, challenges authority, struggles to participate, or disagrees with an established interpretation, what happens?

**Does the community deepen into its principles, or does the tension get resolved through personal preferences and positional authority?**

It's rarely one or the other. Both can be present, sometimes in the same interaction.

And this isn't unique to hierarchy. Even communities organized around autonomy and mutual influence develop informal centers of gravity. When tension rises, what people actually orient around may differ from the principles they share.

## Turning the Mirror

Of course, I can't examine a community without examining my own participation.

I've written about [the moral mirror](/reflections/the-moral-mirror/)—what happens when we turn the principles we expect from others back toward ourselves.

When I feel misunderstood or challenged, am I still practicing what I value? Am I making room for another perspective? Am I becoming attached to my interpretation?

And what happens when I'm the person holding authority?

This is where the [human layer](/reflections/what-i-mean-by-human-layer-engineering/) becomes especially interesting to me.

One person can remain committed to learning through conflict. But **organizational learning requires more than one person's willingness to examine themselves.**

That willingness can be especially consequential among those who influence the conditions of participation.

## Choosing Where to Practice

I've learned that the same community can be a place of growth for one person and a difficult experience for another. Our dispositions, relationships, and circumstances differ.

Some tension contributes to development. Some simply consumes energy. I can't always tell which is happening in the moment, especially when I'm part of the tension. Sometimes I only recognize the difference later, as I reflect on and integrate the experience.

What I'm finding useful is to examine my own part and notice whether there's a shared willingness to deepen the practice, rather than settle things through preference or authority.

**Where there is shared willingness to deepen the practice, I can invest. Where there isn't, I can help cultivate it or choose a different distance.**
