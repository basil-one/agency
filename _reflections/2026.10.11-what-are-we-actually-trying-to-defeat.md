I've heard the expression “All is fair in love and war” used to justify harsh actions, or to normalize what people do to one another when tensions rise.

But I've been wondering about the assumption underneath it.

**Does having the power to hurt someone make using that power wise?**

I practice [Aikido with my children](/reflections/we-walk-together/), and I've been exploring *The Art of Peace*, a collection of teachings from its founder, Morihei Ueshiba, known as O-Sensei.

One distinction I've been reflecting on is the difference between an *enemy* and *enmity*.

An enemy is a person we perceive as opposing us. Enmity is the condition of hostility between people.

That distinction changes the question.

Instead of asking how to defeat someone, we might begin examining what's happening between us. What conditions are sustaining the hostility? How are we participating in it? What might our next action create?

Defeating someone doesn't necessarily resolve enmity. Sometimes it deepens it. The psychological consequences of our actions can ripple through relationships, families, and generations.

In that sense, **we reap what we sow**. Not only through consequences inherited from the past, but through the conditions we're continually creating for what comes next.

Even our best intentions can have harmful impacts. We cannot anticipate every consequence or undo what's already happened. But we can become more aware of our participation and integrate what we're learning.

## When Tension Narrows Our Choices

In heated conversations, I've noticed how quickly we can reach for concepts like fairness, equity, rights, and boundaries.

These are important considerations. But when we're [off-center](https://circle.basil.one/lenses/paradigm/), they can become positions to defend rather than ways to understand what's happening.

Our attention narrows. We become more certain of our interpretation and less capable of seeing the relationship we're participating in.

When pressure fragments a conversation, [Integrate the Conversation](https://circle.basil.one/moves/integrate/) offers a related group practice: holding multiple perspectives and working with tension without forcing premature agreement.

This is where the mindset cultivated through Aikido practice may offer another possibility.

One principle I continue working with is **relax completely**.

Not collapse. Not surrender. Not disengage.

Release unnecessary tension while remaining present and responsive.

Over the years, I've increasingly noticed how this applies beyond the mat.

When we're not in physical danger, we can sit with uncomfortable emotions rather than immediately acting on them. Through reflection and integration, tension can soften, interpretations can loosen, and possibilities we couldn't previously perceive may begin to emerge.

Not because we've forced ourselves to find a solution.

Because we've become more capable of seeing.

**[More options. More agency.](/reflections/the-moral-mirror/)**

## The Art of Peace

Aikido also cultivates sensitivity to *Ma-Ai*—appropriate distance and timing. For me, this extends to tone, intention, and the quality of our engagement.

I'm increasingly skeptical of simple fixes applied universally to complex human relationships.

Sometimes we move closer. Sometimes we need distance. Sometimes we act. Sometimes we allow things to settle.

The principle may remain stable while its expression changes.

None of this guarantees harmony or removes the need for accountability.

But it changes how we participate.

Perhaps the deeper art of peace isn't about defeating an enemy.

**It's about recognizing enmity as something we're participating in, and developing the capacity to respond without unnecessarily reproducing it.**

And that capacity isn't something we suddenly acquire in the middle of conflict.

What if, from the moment conflict arises, we practiced noticing what's happening within us and between us? What if, whenever awareness becomes available, we returned to center, reflected, and integrated what was emerging?

Not as a one-time pause before making a decision, but as an ongoing practice throughout the encounter and beyond it.

Each moment offers another opportunity to become aware, to center, and to choose rather than simply react.

We won't always succeed. Sometimes we'll recognize our reactions only afterward.

But those moments are opportunities for learning too.

**Perhaps the art of peace is something we cultivate moment by moment—expanding our capacity to choose what we contribute to whatever happens next.**
