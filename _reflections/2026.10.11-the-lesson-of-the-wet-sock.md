A wet sock doesn't seem like something that should teach a family much about relationships.

But this weekend, it did.

A small incident involving a wet sock became an opportunity for me and my children, Jack and Lila, to explore something we've been talking about recently: how seemingly minor actions can create consequences we don't anticipate, and how we respond when conflict arises.

I've been exploring *The Art of Peace*, a collection of teachings from Aikido's founder, Morihei Ueshiba, known as O-Sensei. My children and I practice [Aikido together](/reflections/we-walk-together/), and increasingly I'm interested in what that practice teaches us beyond the mat.

One distinction I've been reflecting on is the difference between an *enemy* and *enmity*.

An enemy is someone we perceive as being against us. Enmity is the hostility that develops between people.

That distinction changes where we direct our attention.

Instead of focusing entirely on who is right, who is wrong, or who should be blamed, we can begin examining what's happening in the relationship itself.

What did each person experience? How did our actions affect one another? What are we contributing to what happens next?

## We Reap What We Sow
When I asked Jack what he'd learned from the wet sock, he observed that small things can lead to bigger things, and that it's important to acknowledge our contribution to what happened.

We explored what that might look like.

When someone tells us how our actions affected them, can we listen and reflect back what we heard?

Not necessarily because we agree with their interpretation.

But because **understanding someone's experience and agreeing with their interpretation are different things.**

And the practice works both ways.

Lila offered another perspective. She wanted people to be more careful with their things, but she also shared something important about conflict itself.

She doesn't like being yelled at or having someone's anger directed toward her.

Being able to express that, and having others try to understand it, matters.

Whether she actually feels understood is something time will reveal.

That may have been one of the most important lessons of the conversation.

**Understanding isn't something we can simply declare accomplished. It develops through how we continue treating one another.**

## What Are We Cultivating?
We've all heard the expression “All is fair in love and war.”

I've sometimes heard it used to justify harsh actions or normalize what people do when tension builds.

But having the power to hurt someone, physically or psychologically, doesn't make using that power wise.

Defeating someone doesn't necessarily resolve enmity. Sometimes it creates more of it.

This brings me back to **we reap what we sow**.

We often interpret that expression as a judgment about the past. But it also describes what we're cultivating in the present.

Even our best intentions can have harmful impacts. We can't anticipate every consequence or undo what's already happened.

What we can do is become more aware of our participation, acknowledge the impacts we're learning about, and integrate those lessons into what comes next.

Conflict is inevitable in meaningful relationships.

The question isn't whether we can avoid it entirely.

It's what our participation in conflict is teaching the relationship to become.

If every disagreement produces more blame, defensiveness, avoidance, or resentment, those patterns can accumulate.

But if we practice listening, acknowledging impact, and remaining curious even when we disagree, we're cultivating different conditions for future encounters.

## Practicing Beyond the Mat
One principle I continue working with in Aikido is **relax completely**.

Not collapse, surrender, or disengage.

Release unnecessary tension while remaining present and responsive.

Over the years, I've increasingly noticed how this applies to relationships.

When we're not in physical danger, we can sit with uncomfortable emotions rather than immediately acting on them.

Through [reflection](https://circle.basil.one/lenses/sense/) and [integration](https://circle.basil.one/moves/integrate/), tension may soften, interpretations loosen, and possibilities we couldn't previously perceive begin to emerge.

In heated conversations, concepts like fairness, equity, rights, and boundaries may be important. But when we're [off-center](https://circle.basil.one/lenses/paradigm/), they can also become positions to defend rather than ways of understanding what's happening.

Returning to center doesn't eliminate disagreement.

It changes our relationship to it.

Aikido also cultivates sensitivity to *Ma-Ai*—appropriate distance and timing. For me, this extends to tone, intention, and how we engage with one another.

Sometimes we move closer. Sometimes we need [distance](/reflections/principle-survives-disagreement/). Sometimes we act. Sometimes we allow things to settle.

**[More options. More agency.](/reflections/the-moral-mirror/)**

## The Lesson That Continues
At the end of our conversation, Jack recognized that what we do now can influence what happens in the future, regardless of what brought us to the present moment.

Lila wasn't yet sure what “we reap what we sow” meant to her.

And that's okay.

The purpose wasn't to make everyone arrive at the same interpretation.

It was to practice learning together.

Perhaps that's the deeper lesson of the wet sock.

We can't guarantee that our actions won't cause harm, or that every conflict will end in understanding. We won't always recognize our own contribution in the moment.

But every conflict, and every moment afterward when awareness becomes available, offers another opportunity to reflect, integrate, return to center, and choose how we participate.

**The question isn't simply what happened with the wet sock.**

It's what we're cultivating in our relationships through how we respond.

And what might become possible the next time something small happens?
