It wasn't really about the wet sock.

At least, not by the second time.

On Tuesday, after Aikido, my children Jack and Lila were having dinner when Lila accidentally spilled water. Some landed on Jack, soaking his socks and shoes. Lila got wet too, but Jack was the one left walking home with wet feet.

Then, on Saturday, something similar happened.

After an Aikido demonstration, Jack was washing his foot at a sink. He placed his socks beside it so he could use both hands. Lila came over to wash her feet and placed her socks nearby.

Somehow, one of Jack's socks ended up in the water.

Jack thought Lila's socks had knocked his in. That seemed possible, but none of us knew for certain.

What we did know was that Jack was visibly upset.

Not simply because his sock was wet again, but because he felt that the impact on him wasn't being recognized.

Lila was visibly upset too.

She hadn't intended to cause harm, yet she felt blamed and attacked. Having someone's anger directed toward her was painful, especially when she hadn't meant for any of this to happen.

Both children were experiencing something real.

And each was struggling to understand the other's experience.

## The Enemy Is Not Enmity

I've been exploring *The Art of Peace*, a collection of teachings from Aikido's founder, Morihei Ueshiba, known as O-Sensei.

My children and I [practice Aikido together](/reflections/we-walk-together/), and increasingly I've been reflecting on what that practice teaches us beyond the mat.

One distinction I've been considering is the difference between an *enemy* and *enmity*.

An enemy is someone we perceive as opposing us.

Enmity is the hostility that develops between people.

The distinction matters because it changes where we direct our attention.

If the problem is the other person, we may focus on proving them wrong, defeating them, or making them accept our interpretation.

But if we examine enmity as something developing within a relationship, different questions become available.

What is happening between us? What is each person experiencing? How might our responses be contributing to the conflict?

On Saturday, Jack didn't need Lila to become the problem. He needed his experience to be understood.

And Lila didn't need Jack's frustration to be dismissed. She needed room for her experience to be understood too.

**Understanding one experience doesn't require invalidating another.**

## We Reap What We Sow

During our conversation, I asked Jack what he'd learned.

He said that small things can lead to bigger things, and that it's important to acknowledge our contribution to what happened.

We explored what acknowledging impact might look like.

If someone tells us how our actions affected them, can we listen and reflect back what we heard?

Not necessarily because we agree with every interpretation or accept responsibility for something we didn't do.

But because **understanding someone's experience and agreeing with their interpretation are different things.**

Lila offered another perspective.

She wanted people to be more careful with their things. But she also helped us recognize how painful it can be when someone's anger is directed toward us.

When I asked whether she felt understood, she wasn't entirely sure.

And that's important.

Understanding isn't something we can simply declare accomplished. It develops through how we continue treating one another.

This brings me back to an expression we've been discussing as a family:

**We reap what we sow.**

We often interpret it as a judgment about the past. But it also describes what we're cultivating in the present.

The first wet sock influenced how Jack experienced the second. The second incident became more than an isolated inconvenience because of what had come before.

And how we responded on Saturday could influence how the next disagreement unfolds.

Even our best intentions can have harmful impacts. We cannot anticipate every consequence or undo what's already happened.

But we can become aware of our participation, acknowledge what we're learning about our impact, and integrate those lessons into what comes next.

## When Conflict Narrows Our Choices

I've sometimes heard the expression “All is fair in love and war” used to justify harsh actions or normalize what people do when tension builds.

But possessing the power to hurt someone, physically or psychologically, doesn't make using that power wise.

Defeating someone doesn't necessarily resolve enmity.

Sometimes it deepens it.

In heated conversations, we often reach for concepts like fairness, equity, rights, and boundaries. These may be important considerations, but when we're [off-center](https://circle.basil.one/lenses/paradigm/), they can become positions to defend rather than ways of understanding what's happening.

Our attention narrows.

We become increasingly certain of our interpretation and less capable of seeing the relationship we're participating in.

One principle I continue working with in Aikido is **relax completely**.

Not collapse. Not surrender. Not disengage.

Release unnecessary tension while remaining present and responsive.

Over the years, I've increasingly noticed how this applies beyond the mat.

When we're not in physical danger, we can sit with uncomfortable emotions rather than immediately acting upon them.

Through [reflection](https://circle.basil.one/lenses/sense/) and [integration](https://circle.basil.one/moves/integrate/), tension may soften, interpretations loosen, and possibilities we couldn't previously perceive begin to emerge.

Not because we've forced ourselves to find a solution.

Because we've become more capable of seeing.

**[More options. More agency.](/reflections/the-moral-mirror/)**

Aikido also cultivates sensitivity to *Ma-Ai*—appropriate distance and timing. For me, this extends to tone, intention, and the quality of our engagement.

Sometimes we move closer. Sometimes we need [distance](/reflections/principle-survives-disagreement/). Sometimes we act. Sometimes we allow things to settle.

Returning to center doesn't eliminate conflict or guarantee harmony.

It changes how we participate.

## Practicing for the Next Wet Sock

Toward the end of our conversation, I asked Jack what the lesson of the wet sock might mean for future conflicts.

He recognized that what we do now can influence what happens later, regardless of what brought us to the present moment.

Lila wasn't yet sure what “we reap what we sow” meant to her.

And that's okay.

The purpose wasn't to make everyone arrive at the same interpretation.

It was to practice learning together.

Deep relationships will experience conflict. We'll misunderstand one another. Even our best intentions will sometimes cause harm.

The question isn't whether we can avoid every wet sock.

It's whether we can develop the capacity to recognize what's happening, understand our impact, and return to center when our reactions begin making decisions for us.

And that practice doesn't end when the immediate conflict is over.

Each time awareness becomes available—during the encounter or afterward—we have another opportunity to reflect, integrate what we're learning, and choose how we want to participate.

**We aren't practicing how to avoid the next wet sock.**

**We're practicing who we might become when it arrives.**
