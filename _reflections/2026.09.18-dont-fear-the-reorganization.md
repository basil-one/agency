When someone's relational world changes profoundly—a marriage ends, an identity changes, a family reorganizes, a community disappears—they may experience more than difficult emotions.

Their entire way of organizing reality can become unstable.

And sometimes the destabilizing event is someone else's attempt to become more integrated.

A person makes a decision that brings their life into greater alignment: *This is who I am. This is what I need. I can't continue living this way.*

From inside that person's experience, the change may represent integration.

But the same act can disintegrate a shared reality for someone else.

A marriage, a family, an imagined future, a moral understanding, even a person's understanding of their own life can suddenly stop making sense.

Two experiences can therefore be happening simultaneously.

**One person is reorganizing their life while another is experiencing the collapse of the world that reorganization disrupts.**

## The Signal Underneath the Rupture

We tend to evaluate people's behavior after a rupture without fully appreciating what the rupture itself has changed.

When a foundational reality disappears without someone's agreement, their system may protest.

Sometimes loudly.

There may be anger, pleading, repetition, argument, or intense emotional expression. An enormous signal from a system trying to make sense of something consequential:

**The reality I was living inside is disappearing.**

But people have very different conditioning around what to do with that signal.

Some move toward the rupture. They confront, argue, intensify, demand engagement, and insist that the disruption be acknowledged.

Others move away from the intensity. They withdraw, establish distance, limit contact, or experience the emotional response itself as the central problem.

Neither response is necessarily malicious.

Neither is necessarily healthy.

And together they can become complementary halves of the same destructive loop.

The more one person escalates in an attempt to restore connection or recognition, the more the other withdraws.

The more the other withdraws, the more severe the first person's experience of relational disintegration becomes.

So they escalate further.

And the other retreats further.

Eventually, everyone has evidence that their interpretation was correct.

*See? They won't listen.*

*See? They're out of control.*

*See? They don't care.*

*See? I can't safely engage.*

Each response makes sense from inside the threat that person is experiencing.

But together, they may be making the situation worse.

**Both strategies can become locally rational and collectively destructive.**

At that point, the question isn't only who's behaving badly.

It's also:

**What is happening to the whole system?**

## Storming and Thrashing

This is where I find the distinction between *storming* and *thrashing* useful.

Storming is disorder that participates in reorganization.

Something has broken. Conflict and emotion surface. Previously suppressed realities may become visible. People search for new meanings, boundaries, identities, and ways of relating.

It's uncomfortable, but movement is occurring.

Thrashing is different.

Thrashing consumes enormous amounts of emotional and relational energy without producing greater coherence.

Escalation produces withdrawal. Withdrawal produces escalation. Every person's protective response becomes another person's injury.

Alcohol, deteriorating sleep, and other destabilizing forces can amplify the cycle. Emotional signals grow louder while people's capacity to interpret them and reason together diminishes.

More energy enters the system.

Less useful information gets through.

The distinction matters because **the presence of disorder doesn't tell us whether a system is reorganizing or simply becoming more destabilized.**

We have to look at what the disorder is producing.

## Beyond Diagnosis and Blame

This is one reason I hesitate to immediately pathologize the person who appears most visibly disorganized.

Sometimes clinical support is necessary. Sometimes a person's ability to test their perceptions against reality becomes seriously impaired, and safety must take priority.

But visible distress doesn't tell us everything about what is happening.

A person's response may also be shaped by the collapse of relationships, identities, and shared expectations that previously helped organize their world.

We are more socially constructed than we usually recognize.

Relationships continually help stabilize our sense of who we are, what matters, what is real, and what future we're moving toward.

When those structures disappear, the mind searches for new coherence.

Interpretations multiply. Old explanations stop working. New stories emerge. Patterns that once seemed insignificant may become intensely meaningful.

That doesn't mean every pattern is real or every interpretation is accurate.

It means that understanding the experience may require looking beyond the individual to the relational world that has changed around them.

Clinical care and human connection aren't opposing responses. Both may matter.

And neither understanding someone's distress nor recognizing its context requires other people to abandon necessary boundaries.

## Keeping Reality Permeable

Helping someone through reorganization isn't necessarily arguing them back into their previous understanding of reality.

Nor is it validating everything their mind produces.

Sometimes it means helping reality remain permeable.

Listening. Asking questions. Eating together. Walking. Writing. Creating. Laughing. Doing ordinary things.

Allowing a person's interpretations to remain in contact with other people, other perspectives, and a world that can answer back.

In that sense, being grounded in reality may involve more than maintaining a coherent explanation of what is happening.

It may involve remaining open to experiences that can challenge and reshape that explanation.

Other people can be part of that process.

So can writing, philosophy, art, nature, contemplative practice, and relationships capable of holding complexity without continually increasing its intensity.

Connection isn't always possible or appropriate, and it isn't a substitute for professional care when that care is needed.

But when it is possible, ordinary human participation can help create conditions in which a new sense of coherence begins to emerge.

## Don't Fear the Reorganization

Perhaps the humane response is neither to assume that visible disorganization means someone is simply broken, nor to assume that everything happening is healthy.

It's to become curious about what is reorganizing.

What has been lost? What is trying to emerge? Which responses are helping people regain orientation, and which are amplifying the instability?

Where is storming creating movement?

Where has it become thrashing?

Sometimes the work is to reduce the forces that keep the system activated. Protect sleep, ordinary life, and people's capacity to reason. Maintain boundaries without unnecessarily escalating the conflict. Stay curious without surrendering judgment.

And recognize that the old reality may actually be gone.

The task isn't necessarily to restore it.

It may be to create conditions in which people can develop new ways of understanding themselves, one another, and the relationships that remain possible.

**Don't fear reorganization simply because it looks unfamiliar.**

Learn to distinguish movement toward coherence from cycles that destroy it.

Because sometimes the most important question isn't how to return to the world that existed before.

It's how to remain human to one another while a different world becomes possible.
