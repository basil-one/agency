It is relatively easy to believe in dignity when the person asking for it is someone we understand.

It is easy to believe in fairness when we feel unfairly treated. Easy to believe that context matters when our own behavior is being interpreted without the context we were living through.

The harder question comes when the positions change.

When I believe the other person is wrong. When their behavior frustrates me. When I have authority they do not. When understanding them feels like it might weaken my own position.

That's where a principle becomes something more than an idea.

**It has to survive the disagreement.**

## Dignity Is Not Agreement

One reason this becomes difficult is that we often confuse understanding someone with agreeing with them.

They are not the same thing.

Taking another person's experience seriously doesn't require adopting their interpretation. Treating someone with dignity doesn't require concluding that their behavior was appropriate.

Compassion doesn't require closeness. Understanding doesn't eliminate accountability. Listening doesn't mean surrendering our own experience.

I can believe that my perspective matters and still make room for yours. I can believe that something needs to change and still care about what happens to you in the process.

I can choose greater distance without requiring you to become less human in my mind.

Sometimes the healthiest response to a difficult relationship isn't greater agreement or greater intimacy.

**It's a different distance.**

## Finding Our Center

Aikido has given me a physical language for something I increasingly notice in relationships.

When we lose our center, force begins making decisions for us.

We lean too far into another person. We pull away abruptly. We brace, push, or become preoccupied with controlling their movement because we can no longer feel our own.

Something similar happens emotionally.

Someone threatens our sense of fairness, our identity, or our understanding of what happened. Our attention moves outward: what they did, what they should understand, what they need to change.

And somewhere in that movement, we can lose our own center of gravity.

Recovering it doesn't resolve the disagreement.

It changes our relationship to the disagreement.

I can still be angry. Still believe something was wrong. Still want accountability. Still choose distance.

But those choices no longer have to emerge entirely from the force coming toward me.

I have somewhere else to stand.

## When the Principle Stays and the Distance Changes

The amount of distance a relationship can sustain isn't necessarily a measure of how much we care.

Sometimes closeness supports our capacity to meet another person with dignity. Sometimes it overwhelms that capacity.

The question becomes less:

*How close should we be?*

And more:

**At what distance can I remain centered enough to meet this person as a human being?**

That distance may change as relationships, circumstances, and capacities change.

Sometimes the relationship can recover greater proximity. Sometimes distance must remain. Sometimes the relationship itself may need to end.

But the principle doesn't have to disappear.

Dignity might require listening in one situation, establishing boundaries in another, and holding someone accountable somewhere else.

The principle remains stable.

Its expression adapts.

Rules are easier in this respect. Rules tell us what to do. Principles require us to remain present enough to perceive what the situation is asking from us.

And because that discernment is imperfect, we will sometimes get it wrong.

## Power and the Choice to Escalate

Power makes this more complicated.

When we're affected by someone else's authority, we tend to experience its consequences. When we possess authority, we tend to experience our intentions.

I may know that I'm protecting someone, enforcing an agreement, or establishing a necessary boundary.

Those intentions matter.

But the person on the receiving end experiences what I actually do.

The question isn't only:

**Can I justify what I'm doing?**

It's also:

**What is the force I'm adding entering into?**

Someone may already be frightened, overwhelmed, defensive, or carrying pressures I cannot see.

That doesn't mean abandoning judgment or accountability. It means recognizing that judgment isn't the only capacity available to me.

One of the most consequential forms of power in conflict is the ability to escalate.

We can send the email. Invoke the rule. Call the lawyer. Apply pressure. Demand an answer. Prove the point.

Sometimes escalation is necessary.

But possessing the ability doesn't establish whether using it is wise.

Under pressure, survival can make several possible responses feel like only one: fight, defend, prove, escape, make them understand.

Agency reopens the field.

It gives us enough space to ask:

**What am I actually trying to create here?**

Protection? Repair? Accountability? Understanding? Greater distance? A future relationship? A clean ending?

Those goals don't necessarily require the same response.

Sometimes strength means moving toward. Sometimes it means stepping away.

The important distinction is whether we're choosing our response or allowing the conflict to choose it for us.

## Seeing Our Own Part in the Field

Recovering our center can also reveal something uncomfortable about our own participation.

What felt from the inside like urgency, persistence, or an attempt to restore connection may have been experienced by someone else as pressure.

Our response to distance may have created more distance. Our attempt to resolve a conflict may have helped keep it activated.

Recognizing that doesn't require abandoning our account of what happened.

It asks us to make our account large enough to include our own effect on the field.

There are two mistakes available to us.

One is refusing to see our contribution. The other is believing that seeing it requires condemning everything about ourselves that came before.

Neither seems especially useful.

We can understand where a pattern came from without using that understanding to avoid responsibility. We can acknowledge how our behavior affected others without deciding that everything we intended was false.

We can recognize the good we were trying to do and still become more capable of doing it differently.

That isn't hypocrisy.

It's learning.

## What We Teach When We Disagree

I think about this increasingly as a parent.

Children learn from what we tell them, but they may learn even more from watching what remains available to us when life becomes difficult.

Do our principles disappear when someone angers us? Does having power mean we must use all of it? Can we acknowledge our own mistakes without collapsing into shame? Can we protect someone we love without dehumanizing someone else?

Can distance and care coexist?

These aren't simply lessons about conflict resolution.

They're lessons about what power is for, what accountability looks like, and whether our principles are practices we return to when living by them becomes difficult.

## The Principle Has to Survive the Disagreement

Perhaps that's the test.

Not whether I can articulate a principle, or recognize it when someone fails to extend it to me.

The harder test is whether I can still recognize it from inside the disagreement.

Whether dignity remains dignity when I believe the other person is wrong. Whether restraint remains available when I possess force. Whether accountability can include me without reducing me to my worst moment.

A principle that applies only when it's convenient may be a preference.

A principle that protects only our side may be a strategy.

But if it's something we truly believe about how human beings should treat one another, sooner or later it will have to cross the disagreement.

That doesn't necessarily require us to stand close.

Perhaps it requires something harder:

**To find our center, choose the distance consciously, and see whether the principle is still alive.**
