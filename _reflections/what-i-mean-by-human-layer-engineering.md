When something isn't working, we usually look at the thing.

The strategy. The process. The organizational structure. The technology. The plan.

So we change it.

And sometimes nothing changes.

At that point, it is easy to conclude that the problem is the people.

They are resistant. Leadership is not aligned. Communication is poor. People need to get on board.

Sometimes those descriptions are accurate.

But sometimes what looks like a problem with people is really a problem with the conditions shaping what happens between them.

## The Person or the Environment?

I saw a simple version of this while learning Aikido with my children, a story I've also told in [We Walk Together](/reflections/we-walk-together/).

We were practicing forward roll-outs and repeatedly drifting sideways. An instructor could have kept correcting us:

*Roll straight.*

Instead, one of our instructors placed two foam noodles on the mat to create a lane.

Suddenly the path became visible.

We still had to learn how to roll. The noodles did not do the work for us. But they changed the environment in which we were learning.

Something that looked like a problem with our performance was partly a problem with the conditions surrounding it.

Organizations have environments too.

Some are visible: roles, processes, incentives, technologies.

Others are harder to see: who can disagree with whom, what people believe the work is actually for, whether a decision is really a decision, whether people feel responsible for something or merely assigned to it.

These conditions are not located in any one person.

They emerge between people — from the interpretations they carry, the relationships they inhabit, the conversations they have, and the structures through which they coordinate.

Together, these conditions shape what people are able and willing to do with one another.

I think of this as the **human layer**: the relational conditions through which people's different interpretations, intentions, conversations, and commitments become — or fail to become — collective action.

And **human-layer engineering** is the deliberate shaping of those conditions.

Not engineering people.

**Engineering the conditions in which people participate.**

## Seeing the Water

The difficulty is that much of this layer is invisible, especially to the people inside it.

Groups learn who can say what. Which disagreements are safe. Whose approval everyone quietly waits for. Which ideas gain energy and which disappear.

Eventually nobody has to explain these rules.

People simply move within them.

*A fish does not need a theory of water to swim.*

Neither do we.

The challenge is that we are often inside the conditions we are trying to see.

An initiative can have a sponsor, a strategy, a timeline, and months of meetings — and still be unable to move because people are carrying different interpretations of the problem, avoiding an important disagreement, or acting on decisions that never became shared commitments.

None of that necessarily appears on the project plan.

So the first move in human-layer work is often the same as the noodles:

**Make the conditions visible enough to work with.**

At times, that means slowing the conversation down enough to understand what people are actually seeing.

It may mean naming a disagreement or assumption that everyone is organizing around but nobody has made explicit.

Or it may mean helping a leader notice what they are bringing into the room.

The form can vary: facilitation, coaching, community building, or changes to the formal structure.

But the underlying move is the same:

**Make the conditions shaping collective action visible enough that people can work with them instead of only moving inside them.**

This is why I have come to think of [culture as infrastructure](/reflections/culture-is-infrastructure/).

The human layer is not soft material surrounding the real operating system.

**It is part of the operating system.**

This is also part of what I have been trying to make explicit through [Circle3](https://circle.basil.one/paper): a compact pattern language for creating shared understanding, alignment, and coordinated action.

The patterns do not substitute for the people involved.

Like the noodles, they help make the lane visible.

## Engineering for Agency

The word *engineering* can sound like control.

That is almost the opposite of what I mean.

Human beings are not components to be optimized into compliance.

No facilitator, leader, coach, framework, or methodology can decide for another person whether to participate, listen, speak, reconsider, or take responsibility.

The work is not to determine what people should think.

**It is to create conditions in which people can think together.**

Where different interpretations can become visible.

Where disagreement can remain workable.

Where intention becomes clear enough for people to decide whether and how they want to contribute.

That does not guarantee an outcome.

It changes what becomes possible.

The team may still need to be reorganized. The process may still need to be rewritten. The strategy may still need to change.

But before concluding that the people are the problem, it may be worth asking:

**What conditions are these people trying to act within?**

The noodles did not roll for us.

They made the conditions of the roll visible enough that we could work with them.

Human-layer engineering does something similar.

Not deciding how people should move.

Making more of what shapes their movement visible, so they can decide together what to do with it.

Perhaps that is what human-layer engineering is ultimately for:

**Not greater control over people.**

**Greater agency between them.**
