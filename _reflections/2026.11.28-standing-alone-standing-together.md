There is something deeply rewarding about being part of a group of people who can navigate difficulty together.

Having people with whom we can share our experiences, examine our differences, and find our footing when life becomes difficult opens possibilities that might otherwise remain inaccessible to us.

I've been thinking about what happens when those conditions aren't there.

## When Our Capacities Exceed What a Relationship Can Hold

We can develop our capacity to listen, understand different perspectives, acknowledge our impact, and [work with tension](/reflections/the-world-that-conflict-intelligence-opens/).

What becomes possible together also depends on the capacities and willingness of those around us.

We cannot independently create shared understanding.

We cannot independently repair a relationship.

Sometimes the people we would ordinarily turn toward are part of the difficulty we're navigating.

What capacities become important then?

## Having Somewhere to Stand

One of the things I continue to appreciate about Aikido is the attention given to developing our own center.

When force comes toward us, our ability to remain centered influences what becomes possible.

I increasingly recognize the importance of this beyond the mat.

Can I continue to exercise discernment when the people around me see things differently?

Can I sustain a meaningful direction when the relationships I once depended upon are no longer available?

Can I remain open to another human being without needing them to understand me?

These seem like capacities worth developing in their own right.

## The Ebb and Flow

I've been described at times as having a maverick nature.

In [We Walk Together](/reflections/we-walk-together/), I shared an expression from Chuck Daly that has stayed with me:

*Don't put a saddle on a mustang.*

I can recognize something of myself in that expression.

And some of the most meaningful experiences of my life have come through companionship.

Both are true.

We aren't monoliths. Our temperament, conditioning, experiences, circumstances, and energy all influence how we relate to the world and one another.

I think of the shoreline and how different it can look depending on when we encounter it.

At high tide, the water reaches places that at low tide are exposed to the open air.

Neither is an aberration. Both are expressions of the same sea.

There are periods in our lives when companionship draws us in. There are periods when solitude becomes particularly generative.

Can we recognize these movements without making an identity out of either?

## How We Choose to Participate

We often think about relationships in terms of whether to enter them, remain in them, or leave them.

I've been considering another dimension of that choice.

How do we participate?

There are different degrees of proximity, intimacy, commitment, and interdependence available to us.

The capacity to stand independently may give us greater freedom to explore those possibilities.

We can enjoy what a relationship has to offer without asking it to become something it cannot be. We can care deeply about another person without organizing our entire existence around them.

Companionship introduces complexity into our lives. Sometimes that complexity generates energy, opening possibilities we couldn't have imagined alone. Sometimes it consumes more energy than it generates.

And that, too, can change.

## Walking Together

There are experiences available through solitude that are difficult to encounter in the company of others.

There are experiences available through companionship that we cannot create alone.

I've come to appreciate both.

Perhaps part of learning to walk together is developing the capacity to stand on our own.

**How does our capacity to stand alone influence what becomes possible when we stand together?**
