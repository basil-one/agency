There are times in life when things seem to flow.

An idea takes shape. A conversation opens a possibility. Something that once seemed impossible becomes imaginable. We find ourselves moving with purpose, participating in something meaningful, perhaps even helping shape a different future.

And then there are times when life doesn't cooperate.

An opportunity disappears. An organization resists change. A relationship takes a different course. Our bodies remind us of their limitations. The future that seemed possible yesterday becomes uncertain today.

Life is like a box of chocolates, Forrest Gump famously observed. You never know what you're going to get.

Except sometimes you bite into what looks like chocolate and discover a chocolate-covered rock.

What then?

## The Search for Flow
Much of my professional and personal life has involved exploring how change happens.

Dr. Eliyahu Goldratt's work taught me to look beyond symptoms, understand constraints, and examine the assumptions that keep systems stuck.

George Lakoff's work on metaphor and cognition helped me appreciate how the frames through which we understand reality shape what we perceive as possible.

Alan Watts opened another window into our relationship with life, particularly the paradox of trying to control what we are already participating in.

Through Aikido, the *Tao Te Ching*, and *The Art of Peace* by Morihei Ueshiba, I've explored what it means to meet forces rather than simply resist them.

Other perspectives, including those of David Hawkins and Joe Dispenza, have prompted questions about consciousness, energy, and our relationship with the future. Exploring those perspectives doesn't require accepting all their claims.

I remain something of an agnostic, interested less in defending a particular worldview than in discovering what helps us engage more coherently with reality.

Across these different traditions, I keep returning to a question:

**How do we participate meaningfully in life when we cannot control what happens next?**

When I'm facilitating organizational change, I often work with people to surface impediments, integrate different perspectives, and discover possibilities for moving together.

But individually, the challenge is much the same.

We have intentions. We encounter constraints. We make moves. The circumstances change.

And we begin again.

## When Flow Disappears
It's relatively easy to appreciate a philosophy when life is going well.

The more difficult test comes when we're exhausted, frightened, disappointed, physically uncomfortable, or alone.

I've experienced periods when I felt deeply connected to a mission, only to encounter circumstances that undermined the possibility I was pursuing.

Sometimes the impediments were organizational. Sometimes relational. Sometimes physical.

There have been moments when I've wondered whether I simply needed a better mindset, a stronger spiritual practice, or a clearer vision of the future.

But hardship isn't always a problem of perspective.

Sometimes the constraints are real.

A body needs medical care. An organization has competing interests. Another person makes choices we cannot control. The market may not value what we believe we have to offer.

No amount of positive thinking makes those realities disappear.

And not every difficult experience contains a hidden opportunity waiting to be discovered.

Sometimes life is simply hard.

I don't want a philosophy that only works when things are going well.

I want one that can accompany me through the difficult stretches, too.

## Faith Without Certainty
I've often noticed the comfort some people find in a personal relationship with God.

The belief that someone is looking out for them. That their life has a purpose. That even when circumstances seem overwhelming, they aren't walking alone.

I can appreciate the power of that faith, even though I don't know what to believe about its metaphysical foundations.

My own relationship with spirituality is less easily categorized.

I often sit with the *Tao Te Ching*, reading a single passage and noticing whether I can settle into it.

Sometimes I open *The Art of Peace*.

These writings don't necessarily tell me what to do. They help me recognize where I am.

On some days, I can relax into their wisdom. On others, I notice how difficult it is to do so.

That, too, tells me something.

Perhaps faith doesn't have to begin with certainty about how the universe works.

Perhaps it can begin with a willingness to remain in relationship with life, even when we don't understand what is happening.

Not faith that everything will turn out as we wish.

Not faith that hardship is secretly a gift.

But faith that another step may become possible, even when we cannot yet see it.

## Footprints in the Sand
There's a Christian poem called *Footprints in the Sand*.

In it, a person looks back over their life and notices that during the most difficult periods, there was only one set of footprints.

They wonder why God abandoned them precisely when they needed help most.

The response is that those were the times they were being carried.

My stepfather introduced me to this poem during a difficult period in my life.

My mother married him when I was eighteen. He had his own way of engaging with hardship and relationships. Sometimes connecting with him meant first getting through what I might call [getting the nasties out](/reflections/get-the-nasties-out/).

But he found comfort in Christ, and this poem meant something to him.

He read it at his father's eulogy.

Years later, after he passed, I read it at his casting ceremony.

The same words, carried across generations, through different experiences of loss.

I've been sitting with that image again.

My father died when I was young.

I remember moments when he offered guidance, when he seemed to understand something about where I was and helped me find my way.

After he died, there were people around me. People who cared.

But I also remember the experience of having to send up a signal when I needed help, hoping someone would notice, understand, and respond.

Sometimes they did.

What I remember less often is someone proactively entering my perspective, understanding where I was, and offering guidance from that place.

Perhaps that's part of why the footprints image moves me.

The possibility of being accompanied without having to explain why accompaniment is needed.

Of being understood without first having to make the case.

Of being carried when the strength to continue isn't apparent.

I don't know whether there's a divine presence carrying us through life's difficult passages.

But I know the experience of looking back and wondering how I managed to take the next step.

Where did the energy come from?

How did I find my way through circumstances I couldn't have anticipated?

Was it perseverance? The influence of people who loved me? Some capacity developed through experience? Chance? Grace?

Perhaps some combination.

I don't know.

And maybe I don't need to know.

## Walking, Resting, and Beginning Again
In Aikido, we learn to work with forces rather than simply oppose them.

But this isn't passive acceptance.

There are moments to enter, moments to turn, moments to create distance, and moments to move decisively.

The appropriate response depends on the relationship between ourselves and the circumstances we're encountering.

Life seems much the same.

Sometimes we walk.

Sometimes we jog.

Sometimes we need to sit down and rest.

Sometimes we need help getting back up.

And sometimes we need to recognize that the direction we've been pursuing is no longer available, at least for now.

I think of flow as those periods when our participation in life feels coherent, when our intentions, capabilities, and circumstances come together.

Faith may be what sustains us when that coherence disappears.

And integration may be the ongoing work of bringing our experiences together, learning from them without insisting that every experience must have a lesson.

Writing has become one way I do that.

I begin with a question, an experience, or a contradiction.

I explore the assumptions beneath it.

Sometimes I discover a possibility I hadn't previously considered.

Sometimes I discover a limitation I need to accept.

And sometimes I simply understand myself a little better.

The writing doesn't necessarily change my circumstances.

But it can change my relationship with them.

And occasionally, that opens a move I couldn't see before.

## The Footprints We Leave
There's another dimension to all this that has become increasingly meaningful to me.

My children's mother once told me she wished she could have known my father.

I understand that wish.

I knew my father, but only through certain dimensions of our relationship.

I knew him as my father. I have memories of what he said and did, of moments we shared.

But I don't have much access to his inner world.

What did he wonder about?

What frightened him?

What sustained him through hardship?

How did he understand his place in the world?

I can only imagine.

When I listen to Alan Watts or read the writings of someone who spent time examining their own experience, I sometimes feel as though I can encounter dimensions of that person beyond what a conventional biography might reveal.

Not the entirety of who they were, of course.

But something of how they thought, questioned, and made meaning.

I've come to recognize that my own writings may offer something similar to my children.

Not because I believe my ideas are especially important.

Not because I expect them to adopt my philosophy.

And certainly not because I have life figured out.

Quite the opposite.

These reflections are records of my inquiries, of how I've tried to make sense of life while living it.

The books I've kept, the traditions I've explored, and the things I've written in [Agency](https://agency.basil.one/), [Circle3](https://circle.basil.one/), and [Garden](https://garden.basil.one/) are all expressions of that journey.

My mother has left something of herself in her own words, too. In [Nest](https://garden.basil.one/nest), she writes about her life with nature, the lake, the changing seasons, and her spiritual connection with God's creation.

When her time comes, I intend to read something she wrote.

Perhaps someday, at my own casting ceremony, someone will read one of these reflections.

Not because the words provide answers, but because they offer a glimpse into the person who lived them.

I don't need anyone else to teach my children what these things mean.

I'd simply like them to know they exist.

To know why they mattered to me.

And to be free to explore them, question them, disagree with them, or leave them alone.

Perhaps someday, facing circumstances I could never have imagined, they'll find something that resonates.

Or perhaps they won't.

Either is okay.

These are my footprints, not a path they are obligated to follow.

## The Next Step
I began this inquiry wondering how to reliably experience flow.

How to sustain the energy that comes with purpose, possibility, and meaningful participation.

But perhaps flow cannot be made permanent.

Life changes. Circumstances change. We change.

There are periods of movement and periods of resistance. Times of clarity and times when the way forward disappears.

I don't think the answer is to abandon our intentions or stop imagining better futures.

Nor is it to insist that the universe will arrange itself around those intentions.

We can hold a vision while remaining attentive to reality.

We can examine constraints without surrendering to them.

We can seek help without treating dependence as failure.

We can rest without giving up.

And we can keep asking what move is available now, rather than demanding that the moves we wish were available somehow become real.

Maybe that's where faith, flow, and integration meet.

Not in a promise that life will be easy.

Not in a guarantee that everything happens for a reason.

But in a willingness to remain engaged with life as it unfolds.

Some days we move with confidence.

Some days we struggle to take a single step.

And some days, looking back, we cannot explain how we made it through.

I think about those footprints in the sand.

I don't know whether I was walking, being carried, or somehow both.

I only know that I am here.

And there is another step to take.
