Imagine a soccer match.

Your team has worked the ball up the field. A few clever passes pull the defenders out of position. The goalkeeper commits. The ball comes across the face of the goal.

Two forwards are standing there. The net is wide open.

One says, "We can look at that offline."

The other says, "I'll send you the link."

The ball rolls past both of them.

The crowd stares in disbelief.

The whistle blows. Everyone heads to halftime, enjoys some oranges, and congratulates one another on a productive first half.

Nobody took the shot.

## The Missed Opportunity

Something similar happens when we're trying to move an idea forward.

We bring people together, introduce something promising, and capture their attention.

Someone says, "That's interesting."

And we move on.

But interesting isn't enough.

An idea can make perfect sense without having any meaningful connection to someone's world.

It might be a curiosity. A blinking light. Something worth remembering someday.

Or it might help solve a problem they've been struggling with for months.

**We won't know unless we invite their perspective.**

To explore how the idea connects to their world, we might ask:

- Where might this apply in your world?
- What could it help you accomplish?
- What's in the way?
- Under what conditions would it become worth pursuing?

And then we need to listen.

Not simply for answers, but for what those answers reveal. Interest, hesitation, competing priorities, missing connections, or possibilities we hadn't considered.

We can reflect back what we're hearing, check our understanding, and invite others into the conversation.

This is the [human-layer opportunity](/reflections/what-i-mean-by-human-layer-engineering/).

To discover whether an idea connects to the circumstances people are actually living in, and what it might take to make it real.

## Push Less. Pull More.

When we care about an idea, our instinct is often to push.

Explain it. Demonstrate it. Document it. Distribute it.

Sometimes that's exactly what's needed.

But information flowing outward is only half the exchange.

**We need feedback flowing inward.**

Not merely whether people understand, but what the idea means in their context. Whether there's a reason to care. What might make it useful, difficult, or worth pursuing.

As perspectives emerge, we can invite the group to explore further:

- What connects?
- What doesn't?
- What might need to change?
- What are we learning from one another?

And when those perspectives come onto the table, we can explore and integrate them together.

The conversation becomes a feedback loop.

What we learn changes our understanding of the idea, the conditions surrounding it, and what might be possible next.

Sometimes we discover genuine interest. Sometimes an impediment. Sometimes the idea itself needs to change.

And sometimes we discover that it doesn't belong in that context at all.

Each discovery brings the idea into closer contact with reality and helps us find our way forward.

## Take the Shot

Ideas can [die on the vine](/reflections/when-the-idea-dies-on-the-vine/) even when everyone understands them.

Understanding isn't the same as willingness to invest.

And willingness isn't something we can push onto people.

We have to discover what might make it possible.

When someone gives us their attention, we have an opening.

We can give them something to think about later.

Or we can become curious about what they're experiencing now.

**The shot isn't getting them to agree. It's inviting the feedback that helps us discover where an idea might take root, and what it would take to bring it to life.**

The next time the ball comes across the goal, will we recognize the opportunity?

Or will we head to halftime for oranges?
