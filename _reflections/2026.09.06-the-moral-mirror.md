We are remarkably good at recognizing fairness when we need someone else to practice it.

When someone holds power over us, we understand restraint. When our circumstances go unseen, we understand compassion. When someone makes a decision that affects us, we want them to consider our position.

*They should understand. They should be careful. They should not act simply because they can.*

And perhaps we're right.

But the moral mirror asks us to turn it around.

**Can I recognize the same principle when I'm the one holding power?**

## Turning the Mirror

We move between positions throughout our lives.

Sometimes we need understanding. Sometimes someone needs it from us. Sometimes we experience another person's authority. Sometimes we're the one exercising it.

Yet something changes when we cross that line.

**When we're subject to power, we experience its consequences. When we exercise power, we experience our intentions.**

I know why I'm enforcing a decision, protecting myself, setting a boundary, or pursuing an opportunity.

But someone else lives with what my action creates.

My intentions matter. So do their consequences.

The mirror doesn't ask us to pretend every situation is equal, or to surrender our judgment.

It asks us to examine the principle from the other side.

*Would I still recognize it if our positions were reversed?*

## Seeing the Whole Field

[Aikido offers a physical expression of this](/reflections/we-walk-together/).

In randori, one person faces several attackers. Each attacker may see only their own encounter. The person in the center experiences the entire field.

A force that seems small from one position can be overwhelming from another.

In conflict, we rarely see every force acting on someone else. We see our interaction, our reasons, our intentions. We may not see everything they're carrying.

And when we feel threatened, our own field narrows.

We defend. We resist. We withdraw. We insist.

**Survival compresses choice.**

The practice of Aikido isn't to become impossible to overwhelm. It's to recover our center, recognize the forces in motion, and discover responses beyond opposing force with force.

The moral mirror invites a similar practice.

Before acting, can I see more of the field?

Before judging, can I turn the mirror?

Before insisting I'm right, can I recognize what my position keeps me from seeing?

## A Down Payment on Agency

We won't always manage this under pressure.

That's why we [practice before we need it](/reflections/what-i-mean-by-human-layer-engineering).

We revisit moments when we needed understanding, held authority, or felt wronged. Then we turn the mirror.

Not to retry the past. Not to decide who was good.

To recognize a principle we might someday need to practice from another position.

Each turn makes another perspective available. Another possible response. Another place to stand.

And perhaps, when pressure returns, we'll recognize the moment before survival chooses for us.

A grievance can become something more enduring:

*You should not have done this to me.*

Becomes:

*This is something we should be careful about doing to one another.*

And the mirror turns once more.

**Where am I being asked to practice that principle now?**

Each time we recognize it, we make a small down payment on future agency.

A little more awareness. A little more restraint. A little more freedom to choose.

Not just when we need someone else to see us.

But when we're the one holding the mirror.
