## Get the Sillies Out
During our family Aikido classes at [Tenshinkan Dojo](https://japaneseculturecenter.com/classes/aikido), one of our instructors sometimes invites the children to "get the sillies out" before training.

It's a playful invitation. Rather than demanding that the children immediately settle down, the instructor makes room for the energy that's already present.

There's wisdom in that simple practice.

But what happens when the energy isn't playful?

What about frustration, disappointment, resentment, or anger?

**What if we could get the nasties out, too?**

## When Energy Comes Out Sideways
Sometimes difficult emotions find their way into our interactions.

A playful comment becomes a personal jab. Disappointment becomes withdrawal. Resistance becomes distraction.

I've experienced this from both sides.

When I feel targeted, I notice myself becoming defensive, questioning intentions, and withdrawing trust.

And when I'm trying to help someone engage, my encouragement can become persistence. What feels like an invitation to me may feel like pressure to someone else.

Before long, we're reacting to one another rather than recognizing what's happening between us.

## Working With What's Present
One of the principles I continue exploring through Aikido is *blending*—learning to harmonize with an incoming force rather than simply opposing it.

Harmony, as I've come to understand it, isn't necessarily the absence of conflict. It's something that can emerge through how forces meet.

Perhaps "get the nasties out" could be a playful invitation to practice something similar.

*Something bothering you? Frustrated? Disappointed? Bring it. Let's find a way to work with it.*

Not by turning our frustration against one another, but by acknowledging what's present and discovering what becomes possible.

Sometimes that means talking. Sometimes moving. Sometimes allowing a little space.

The invitation matters. So does the freedom to receive it.

## Beyond the Mat
I see this in families and workplaces, too.

We arrive carrying pressures, disappointments, and unfinished conversations that others cannot necessarily see.

When that energy surfaces, we often try to correct the behavior, defend ourselves, or regain control.

Sometimes that's necessary.

But perhaps there's another move available.

**When difficult energy enters the room, what energy do I bring to meet it?**

I've begun to think that "get the sillies out" and "get the nasties out" are two expressions of the same underlying practice: meeting people where they are.

I'm inspired to experiment with this in my personal and professional circles when the moment seems right. Not as a technique for changing how others feel, but as an invitation to work with what's already present.

We won't always arrive centered, cheerful, or ready to participate.

Perhaps we don't need to.

**Maybe we don't need everyone to arrive with good energy.**

**Maybe we need to practice cultivating it together.**
