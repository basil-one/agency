Imagine you've been working with colleagues to improve something in your organization. You've experienced the problem, explored alternatives, and developed a promising proposal.

The early conversations go well. People recognize the problem. Some are enthusiastic; others have concerns. You begin to imagine something might actually change.

But as the idea approaches reality, progress becomes harder. Other priorities take precedence. Decisions get deferred. Objections linger. Perhaps nobody rejects the proposal, but the conversations gradually stop.

The idea dies on the vine.

Perhaps you've experienced this. I certainly have.

It's frustrating, especially when you've invested so much of yourself in something you believe matters. It's tempting to conclude that we need stronger sponsorship, better communication, or more persistence. Sometimes we do. But perhaps something more fundamental is happening.

**Understanding a problem isn't the same as feeling it's worth solving. And agreeing with a solution isn't the same as being willing to invest in it.**

## Making Room for Change

The present has a powerful advantage. People know how to operate within it. They've developed relationships, routines, and ways of navigating its constraints.

These patterns aren't merely familiar. They're continually reinforced by what gets recognized, rewarded, and demanded. Even when people agree that change is needed, the surrounding system may make staying the course the sensible choice.

For something new to take root, something else has to make room.

You, me, enough of us—we need to become willing and able to say no, or at least *not now*, to something we're already doing. That's difficult when today's priorities already consume our attention.

Our challenge isn't simply to win approval. It's to discover a future enough people will consider worth investing in, and what would make that investment possible.

And that may require us to loosen our grip on the idea we've worked so hard to develop.

We won't discover that future from our perspective alone.

## Finding Our Way Through

We need to venture into the [human layer](/reflections/what-i-mean-by-human-layer-engineering/). Seek people out, listen to what matters to them, bring people together, and return to conversations where understanding hasn't yet emerged.

This isn't simply persuasion. Someone who appears resistant may be revealing a constraint, a competing priority, or an assumption we've overlooked. Their behavior may make perfect sense within the system they're navigating.

What if, instead of asking why they won't support our idea, we become curious about what we haven't yet understood?

Fortunately, we have tools to help us explore that question.

Eliyahu Goldratt's systems thinking helps us distinguish symptoms from core problems, uncover underlying conflicts, and challenge assumptions. Christopher Alexander's pattern thinking offers a useful structure for making our understanding visible:

- **Context:** What situation are we in?
- **Problem:** What difficulty are we trying to address?
- **Forces:** What needs, tensions, conflicts, and assumptions shape it?
- **Solution:** What might work?
- **Resulting Context:** What happens when we apply the solution?

AI can help us recognize patterns, surface contradictions, and organize what we're learning into a working map. But understanding remains something we develop with people, testing and revising that map together.

And when we get stuck, [*Fearless Change*](https://fearlesschangepatterns.com/), by Mary Lynn Manns and Linda Rising, offers patterns for building engagement and momentum. [*Circle3: Three Guiding Moves for Leading Difficult Change*](https://circle.basil.one/paper) offers another way to sustain shared attention, integrate perspectives, and reconcile intentions.

These are resources for navigating change, not recipes for imposing it.

## Bringing Change to Life

The real work happens in our encounters with people.

As we listen, challenge assumptions, and test what we're learning, our understanding evolves. The map becomes clearer, and through reflection and conversation, a shared intention can emerge as our compass.

The idea we started with may not be the one that ultimately takes root. But together, we may discover something better suited to the world we're trying to influence.

Perhaps the next step isn't to make another case for our idea, but to return to a conversation with someone who sees things differently—and discover what we've been missing.

**Change is hard. Let's shape it together.**
