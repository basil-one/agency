Have you ever walked away from a conversation thinking, *Here we are again*?

Perhaps you've tried to approach a difficulty differently, explain yourself more clearly, or understand another person's perspective.

And yet, somehow, you find yourselves back in familiar territory.

Something keeps happening.

Recognizing that recurrence is a beginning. But what kind of pattern are we encountering? What sustains it? And how might we work with it?

A recent conversation with a Zen master brought me back to these questions.

Patterns are central to this [approach to life](/reflections/life-is-an-inquiry-not-an-accumulation/). But not all patterns are of the same nature.

And that distinction matters.

## Patterns We Embody

Through personal exploration and leadership coaching, I've become increasingly interested in the relationship between how we think, feel, and engage with the world.

What drew me to the [Focus Energy Balance Indicator (FEBI®)](https://zenleader.global/resources/febi-assessment) is that it integrates mind and body by design. This makes sense given its [origins in the study of neuromuscular patterns](https://zenleader.global/s/FEBI-Technical-Manual-2024.pdf).

FEBI offers a language for four energy patterns—Driver, Organizer, Collaborator, and Visionary.

Each brings something different: the capacity to move things forward, bring order to a situation, connect with others, or open ourselves to new possibilities.

What I've come to appreciate is that we can do more than recognize our preferences. We can cultivate our capacity to access different patterns as circumstances call for them.

Imagine entering a conversation determined to resolve something. You recognize that what's needed is less driving and more listening. Intellectually, you understand this. But you can still feel the urgency in your body.

Knowing how we'd like to respond and being able to respond that way are different capabilities.

Sometimes the opening for change comes through reflection. Other times, it comes through movement, breath, or a shift in how we're carrying ourselves.

## Patterns We Participate In

Of course, how we respond is only part of what's happening in that conversation.

In developing the [leadership lenses for Circle3](https://circle.basil.one/lenses/), I've found it useful to explore three dimensions of our experience:

- **Sense Making:** How are we interpreting the situation?
- **Energy Awareness:** How are we engaging with it?
- **Paradigm Awareness:** Are we oriented toward protection, openness, or possibility?

Each helps us notice something we might otherwise overlook.

But our responses also influence one another.

Perhaps the harder I push for resolution, the more you withdraw. And the more you withdraw, the harder I push.

Over time, we develop a familiar way of relating to one another.

Similar patterns emerge in families, teams, and organizations. The expectations, constraints, and shared histories surrounding us can continually reinforce them.

Sometimes the difficulty we're experiencing isn't something within us that needs to change.

It's something between us that needs to be understood, or something around us that needs to change.

## Patterns That Bring Life

Another influence on this approach is the architect Christopher Alexander.

Alexander explored recurring problems, the forces that shape them, and ways of resolving them within their context.

But what draws me to his work goes beyond the structure of a pattern. It's his interest in what gives things life.

Think of a place where you naturally want to linger. Perhaps a courtyard, a neighborhood café, or a room where people seem to come together effortlessly.

What is it about that place that supports the life unfolding within it?

Alexander's work invites us to notice these qualities, discover the patterns that give rise to them, and participate in creating living structures of our own.

I find something of that spirit in the [PLoP community](https://plopcon.org/), where people come together to discover, articulate, and share patterns drawn from their experiences.

These patterns offer us something beyond an understanding of what already happens. They allow us to learn from one another about what works, under what conditions, and what we might cultivate in our own circumstances.

Not as recipes to follow, but as possibilities to bring into practice.

## An Embodied Mindset

When I speak of *pattern thinking*, I'm not referring to an exclusively intellectual activity.

What I'm cultivating is an embodied mindset—a way of perceiving and engaging with the world through mind and body together.

This is one of the reasons I practice Aikido at [Tenshinkan Dojo](https://japaneseculturecenter.com/classes/aikido).

On the mat, I can understand the principle of working with forces rather than opposing them. But when someone actually applies force, I discover how deeply I've integrated that understanding.

And I encounter that same distinction away from the mat, in relationships, conversations, and the work of leading change.

Through practice, what we understand can gradually become part of how we meet the world.

## Finding an Opening

Returning to that familiar conversation, what might become possible?

Perhaps there's an assumption I need to examine. Perhaps I need to work with the tension I'm carrying in my body. Perhaps we need a different way of relating to one another, or a change in the conditions surrounding us.

The nature of the pattern matters because it helps us discover where and how we might influence it.

And not every pattern needs to change.

We can also become curious about the moments when a conversation flows, when understanding emerges, or when people find themselves moving together.

What made that possible?

**Perhaps patterns matter most when they help us recognize what brings life and develop our capacity to cultivate it.**
