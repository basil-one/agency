I've been thinking about neurodivergence, and a question keeps getting in the way.

**Divergence from what?**

There are real neurological differences between people. But when we describe someone as divergent, we're also invoking a reference point. What makes that reference point normal? And how much of what we consider normal human behavior reflects the cultures we've inherited rather than human nature itself?

## Seeing the Water

A fish doesn't need a theory of water to swim. Neither do we.

We enter families, schools, workplaces, and communities long before we can examine their assumptions. We learn what earns approval, what creates belonging, and which behaviors are rewarded. Eventually, participation becomes automatic.

But once we begin to see the water, something changes.

We can ask whether our behavior reflects what we value or simply what we've learned to perform. Expectations that once felt natural become cultural arrangements we can consciously evaluate.

**Awareness doesn't necessarily change our ability to participate. It changes our relationship to participation.**

What looks like disengagement from inside the group may represent increasing agency for the individual.

## Disposition or Divergence?

This makes me wonder whether *disposition* is sometimes a more useful starting point than *divergence*.

Divergence measures difference from a norm. Disposition asks about someone's underlying orientation toward the world.

A person inclined to question assumptions might thrive in a learning community and struggle where loyalty means not questioning authority. The disposition hasn't changed. The environment has changed what that disposition means.

This doesn't invalidate neurological diagnoses or the real challenges some differences create. But it suggests that some apparent difficulties may reflect a mismatch between a person's disposition and the conditions of participation.

## When Belonging Depends on the Role

I've experienced something related through social ejection.

There were relationships where my behavior became part of the justification for exclusion. Some criticisms were legitimate. I took responsibility and worked to change those behaviors, believing that accountability might create a path toward reintegration.

But when the behaviors changed, meaningful reintegration wasn't offered. The reasons for maintaining distance were no longer necessarily moral. People simply didn't want the relationship anymore.

And people are allowed to make that choice.

What changed was my understanding of belonging. I had assumed it was organized around shared principles, accountability, and repair. Perhaps it depended more on social roles and preferences than I'd recognized.

I began to wonder whether I had ever been integrated as a person, or whether only the role I performed had been accepted.

Once that distinction became visible, performing the role was no longer automatic.

## The Organizing Logic of Normal

Families, corporations, and social groups all develop expectations about acceptable participation. Over time, those expectations become normal.

But **normal doesn't necessarily mean natural, healthy, or virtuous.**

Groups don't just shape behavior. They shape how behavior is interpreted. Someone who questions assumptions becomes difficult. Someone who stops performing an expected role becomes uncooperative.

Sometimes those judgments are accurate. But sometimes the person hasn't lost the capacity to participate. They've become aware of what participation requires and no longer accept the exchange.

Awareness isn't automatically wisdom, and nonconformity isn't automatically healthy. But the distinction deserves attention.

**What looks like growing divergence from the outside may sometimes be growing self-integration from the inside.**

A person may still know how to perform the role. They may simply no longer believe it's worth performing.

That isn't necessarily dysfunction. It may be discernment.

So before asking why someone diverges from the norm, perhaps we should ask:

**What is the organizing logic of the norm we're asking them to conform to?**
