I've been thinking about neurodivergence, and a question keeps getting in the way.

**Divergence from what?**

There are real neurological differences between people. Some affect attention, sensory processing, communication, and how we navigate the world. But when we describe someone as divergent, we're also invoking a reference point.

What makes that reference point normal? And how much of what we consider normal human behavior is actually the product of cultures that have become particularly successful at organizing people?

I'm not convinced we always distinguish between human nature and the organizing logic of the cultures we've inherited.

## Seeing the Water

A fish doesn't need a theory of water to swim. Neither do we.

We enter families, schools, workplaces, and communities long before we can examine their assumptions. We learn what earns approval, what creates belonging, which behaviors are rewarded, and which questions are unwelcome.

Eventually, participation becomes automatic. The environment teaches us how to move within it.

But once we begin to see the water, something changes.

We can ask whether the behaviors we've learned actually reflect what we value. We can recognize expectations that previously felt natural as cultural arrangements we never consciously chose.

Awareness doesn't necessarily change our ability to participate. It changes our relationship to participation.

And sometimes, once we understand what a system is asking of us, we become less willing to comply.

From inside the system, that may look like disengagement. From inside the individual, it may represent increasing agency.

## Disposition or Divergence?

This makes me wonder whether *disposition* is sometimes a more useful starting point than *divergence*.

Diversity describes variation. Divergence measures difference from a norm. Disposition asks about someone's underlying orientation toward the world.

Some people are disposed toward exploration, others toward stability. Some toward independent judgment, others toward social coordination.

A person inclined to question assumptions might thrive in a learning community and struggle in an organization where loyalty means not questioning authority.

The disposition hasn't changed. The environment has changed what that disposition means.

This doesn't invalidate neurological diagnoses. Some differences create meaningful challenges across environments, and understanding them can provide essential support.

But it does suggest that what appears to be an individual difficulty may sometimes be a mismatch between a person's disposition and the conditions of participation.

And awareness can make that mismatch increasingly difficult to ignore.

## When Belonging Depends on the Role

I've experienced something related through social ejection.

There were relationships where my behavior became part of the justification for exclusion. Some criticisms were legitimate. I had things to own, patterns to understand, and behaviors to change.

I worked on them, believing accountability and change would create the possibility of reintegration.

But when the behaviors changed, meaningful reintegration wasn't offered. The reasons for maintaining distance were no longer necessarily the original moral reasons. People simply didn't want the relationship anymore.

And people are allowed to make that choice.

What changed was my understanding of the group's organizing logic.

I had believed belonging was organized around shared principles, accountability, and repair. Perhaps it depended more on social roles, preferences, and relationships than I had recognized.

I began to wonder whether I had ever been integrated as a person, or whether the role I performed had been integrated.

That experience made something visible: belonging can depend less on who we are than on our willingness and ability to perform what a group expects.

And once we recognize that exchange, performing the role becomes a different kind of choice.

## The Organizing Logic of Normal

Families, corporations, communities, and social groups all develop organizing logics. They establish expectations, reward certain behaviors, and reinforce particular ways of participating.

Over time, those patterns become normal.

But normal doesn't necessarily mean natural, healthy, or virtuous.

Human beings have powerful capacities for imitation and cooperation. Yet the particular norms we imitate may simply belong to cultures that became effective at reproducing themselves, coordinating behavior, and maintaining influence.

**Successful cultural reproduction is not the same thing as moral legitimacy.**

This matters because groups don't only influence behavior. They influence how behavior is interpreted.

The person who questions assumptions becomes difficult. The person who resists an expected role becomes uncooperative. The person who no longer responds to conventional rewards appears unmotivated.

Sometimes those interpretations are accurate.

But sometimes the person hasn't lost the capacity to participate. They've become aware of the conditions attached to participation and no longer accept them.

That distinction matters.

## What Are We Actually Measuring?

When someone struggles to participate, we tend to examine the person.

Their motivation. Their behavior. Their ability to adapt.

Those questions can be useful, but they're incomplete without examining the environment.

What is this system asking people to become? Which dispositions does it accommodate? What happens when someone questions its assumptions? Does belonging require performing a particular identity?

Awareness isn't automatically wisdom, and nonconformity isn't automatically healthy. We can misread systems just as easily as we can participate in them unconsciously.

But increased awareness can make previously invisible expectations available for examination.

And that creates a possibility we should take seriously.

**What looks like growing divergence from the outside may sometimes be growing self-integration from the inside.**

A person may still know how to perform the role. They may simply no longer believe the role is worth performing.

That isn't necessarily dysfunction. It may be discernment.

So perhaps before asking why someone diverges from the norm, we should ask a more fundamental question:

**What is the organizing logic of the norm we're asking them to conform to?**
