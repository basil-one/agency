When someone's relational world changes profoundly — a marriage ends, an identity changes, a family reorganizes, a community disappears — sometimes the person doesn't simply experience difficult emotions.

Their whole way of organizing reality can become unstable.

And sometimes the destabilizing event is actually someone else's attempt to become more integrated.

A person makes a decision that brings their own life into greater alignment: This is who I am. This is what I need. I can't continue living this way. I'm leaving.

From inside that person's experience, the change may represent integration.

But the same act can disintegrate a shared reality for someone else.

A marriage, a family, a future, a moral understanding, a sense of who another person was, and even a person's understanding of their own life can suddenly stop making sense.

Two experiences can therefore be happening simultaneously:

One person is reorganizing their life while another person is experiencing the collapse of the world that reorganization disrupts.

## The Signal Underneath the Rupture

That matters because we tend to evaluate people's behavior after the rupture without fully appreciating the rupture itself.

When a foundational reality disappears without someone's agreement, their system may protest.

Sometimes loudly.

Pain comes through tone. Emotion comes through language. There may be intensity, repetition, anger, pleading, argument, dramatic expression: an enormous signal being generated by a system saying:

**Something consequential is happening here. The reality I was living inside is disappearing.**

But then something else happens.

People have very different conditioning around what to do with that signal.

Some people move toward rupture: confront it, argue, intensify, demand engagement, insist that the disruption be acknowledged.

Others move away from intensity: withdraw, shut down, establish distance, minimize contact, or experience the intensity itself as the central problem.

Neither response is necessarily malicious.

And neither response is necessarily healthy.

Because they can become complementary halves of the same feedback loop.

The more one person escalates in an attempt to restore connection or recognition, the more the other withdraws.

The more the other withdraws, the more severe the first person's experience of relational disintegration becomes.

So they escalate further.

And the other retreats further.

Eventually everyone has evidence that their interpretation was correct.

*See? They won't listen.*

*See? They're out of control.*

*See? They don't care.*

*See? I can't safely engage with them.*

A social and relational death spiral has formed.

At that point, asking which "tribe" has the healthier instinct may miss something important. Both strategies can become locally rational and collectively destructive.

Each person is responding to what is immediately threatening to them.

But neither can step far enough outside the loop to ask:

**What is happening to the whole system?**

## Storming and Thrashing

This is where the distinction between *storming* and *thrashing* becomes important.

Storming is disorder that participates in reorganization. Something has broken. Conflict and emotion occur. Previously suppressed realities may surface. People search for new meanings, boundaries, identities, and forms of relationship. It is uncomfortable, but movement is occurring.

Thrashing is different.

Thrashing is when enormous amounts of emotional and relational energy are being consumed without producing greater coherence.

Escalation produces withdrawal. Withdrawal produces escalation. Every person's protective response becomes another person's injury.

Alcohol and other destabilizing forces can dramatically increase the amplitude. Sleep deteriorates. Interpretations multiply. Emotional signals become larger. People's capacity to reason together decreases.

More energy enters the system.

Less useful information gets through.

## The Risk of Only Pathologizing

This is one reason I hesitate to immediately pathologize the person who appears most visibly disorganized.

Sometimes clinical help really is necessary. Sometimes someone's ability to test their perceptions against reality has become seriously impaired, and safety has to come first.

But making sickness our only frame can itself alter the social system.

People become afraid. They distance themselves. They stop engaging. They hand the person over to professionals.

And yet relationship may be one of the mechanisms through which human beings regain orientation.

We are more socially constructed than we usually recognize. Relationships continuously stabilize our sense of who we are, what matters, what is real, and what future we're moving toward.

When those structures collapse, the mind searches.

Patterns appear everywhere. Symbols become powerful. Old explanations stop working. New stories proliferate.

That doesn't mean every pattern is real.

It doesn't mean every new story is true.

It means the system is searching.

## Reality Has to Remain Permeable

Helping, then, isn't necessarily arguing someone back into normality.

And it isn't validating everything their mind produces.

It can mean remaining in relationship while helping reality remain permeable.

Listening. Asking questions. Eating together. Sleeping. Walking. Writing. Creating. Laughing. Doing ordinary things. Allowing another human mind to remain present while a person's world reorganizes.

In that sense, being attached to reality may not mean never becoming strange, emotional, disorganized, or intensely searching.

It may mean remaining in relationship with a world that can answer back.

Other people are part of that world.

There are also ways of going deeply into human experience without continually increasing the amplitude: writing, philosophy, art, conversation, silence, spiritual practice, nature, relationships capable of holding complexity.

## Neither Diagnosis Nor Denial

Perhaps the humane stance, then, is neither:

**This person is sick. Distance yourself.**

nor:

**Everything happening here is healthy.**

It is something harder:

Something is reorganizing.

Don't fear it simply because it looks strange.

Stay connected without surrendering your boundaries.

Stay curious without agreeing with everything.

Reduce the things that turn storming into thrashing.

Protect sleep, the body, ordinary life, and people's ability to reason together.

And when conflict becomes a feedback loop, stop asking only which person is behaving badly — start asking what each person's behavior is doing to the whole system.

Because sometimes the task isn't to restore the old reality.

The old reality may actually be gone.

The task is to remain human to one another long enough for a new reality to become coherent enough to be shared.
