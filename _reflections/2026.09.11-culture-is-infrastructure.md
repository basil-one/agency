For several years, I served on the Board of a small condominium association, including as president.

During that time, we worked with three different professional property management companies. All three were reputable. All three had capable people. And each time, at least initially, there were reasons to believe the experience might be different.

But many of the same problems kept returning.

Longstanding conflicts remained unresolved. Building issues survived changes in management. Responsibilities sometimes became less clear rather than more clear.

An owner would approach the property manager and discover that something required a Board decision. They would approach the Board and hear that management was supposed to handle it.

The manager might reasonably operate within its scope. The Board might reasonably believe it had delegated the work. And the problem could remain unresolved while everyone involved sincerely believed someone else was responsible for moving it forward.

Eventually, I began to suspect that the strongest variable wasn't the management company.

It was the culture and leadership of the association itself.

## Adding Capability Isn't Transferring Responsibility

When a system becomes difficult to manage, one of our natural instincts is to add another layer.

A manager. A consultant. A process. A platform. Another layer of expertise.

Sometimes that's exactly the right response.

But **adding capability and transferring responsibility are not the same thing.**

Professional management companies need defined scopes, repeatable processes, and enough standardization to serve multiple clients. That isn't a criticism. It's how the model works.

But every organization also has its own history, relationships, unwritten expectations, and unresolved tensions.

A property manager can administer a system. It cannot substitute for a functioning system of governance.

Eventually, we tried a different approach.

Rather than relying on one intermediary to hold everything together, we built direct relationships with the professionals whose expertise we actually needed: bookkeepers, attorneys, insurance professionals, tradespeople, and service vendors.

We also became more intentional about matching people within the building to roles where their skills and interests could contribute.

The lesson wasn't that professional management was inherently wrong. It was that we had been asking management to solve problems that belonged to the association itself.

Expertise could be purchased. Administrative work could be delegated.

But the system still needed to own its decisions, relationships, and responsibilities.

## Culture Is Infrastructure

For a long time, I thought of culture as something adjacent to governance.

Relationships mattered. Trust helped. Social events were nice. But the real work was budgets, contracts, insurance, maintenance, rules, and decisions.

I no longer see those things as separate.

The social fabric was part of the infrastructure through which governance happened.

Knowing one another helped us understand who had relevant expertise, who cared about particular issues, where tensions existed, and who might be willing to contribute.

It created opportunities for direct conversations rather than allowing every disagreement to disappear behind email, enforcement, or institutional intermediaries.

Those conversations weren't always comfortable. But problems that had survived for years finally began to move.

I've come to believe that an organization continually teaches people how to participate within it.

Who can question a decision? Where does responsibility live? Is disagreement safe? Will reasoning be visible? Does speaking up lead to understanding or escalation?

These lessons rarely need to be written into a policy.

People learn them by participating in the system.

**Culture is infrastructure.**

It isn't the soft material surrounding the operating system. It's part of the operating system.

A sophisticated strategy operating inside an unhealthy culture will eventually be shaped by that culture. A relatively simple system operating inside a culture of openness, responsibility, and trust can accomplish an extraordinary amount.

## When Responsibility Disappears

One of the more interesting things about systems is that they can contain many responsible people and still produce irresponsibility.

The manager protects its scope. The Board assumes administration is being handled. The owner assumes someone with authority is addressing the problem. The vendor waits for instruction.

From inside each role, the behavior may make perfect sense.

And still nothing happens.

The failure belongs less to any individual than to the architecture through which responsibility is moving.

That's why governance can't simply mean assigning tasks.

Someone has to know:

**Who owns this? Who decides? Who advises? Who executes? Who needs to understand why?**

Without that clarity, accountability has nowhere reliable to land.

And without relationships that allow people to navigate those responsibilities together, even a carefully designed structure can become another place for work to stall.

## Make the Reasoning Visible

Transparency became another important part of the lesson.

People don't only need to know what was decided. They need enough visibility into how the decision was made.

What information was available? What constraints existed? What alternatives were considered? Where did judgment enter? Why was one path chosen over another?

When that reasoning remains invisible, people fill in the gaps with explanations drawn from their own histories, assumptions, and experiences.

Transparency doesn't guarantee agreement.

**It gives disagreement something real to organize around.**

Instead of *I don't trust what they're doing*, the conversation can become *I understand how they reached the decision, and I disagree with this part of their reasoning.*

That's a very different kind of conflict.

This matters even more as our informational environment changes.

AI increasingly allows ordinary participants to organize and examine complex governing documents, correspondence, policies, and institutional histories. That doesn't make every interpretation correct or replace professional judgment. But it changes how accessible these systems can become.

Complexity is becoming less effective as a barrier to understanding.

The healthier response is to make the system more legible: share information, explain decisions, acknowledge uncertainty, and allow people to inspect the reasoning.

The goal isn't to eliminate disagreement.

It's to make disagreement more workable.

## A Culture That Can Carry Disagreement

Healthy governance doesn't require everyone to become equally enthusiastic about participating.

A few people with complementary skills, working well together, can change the trajectory of an organization.

The goal isn't maximum involvement. It's enough healthy participation that responsibility doesn't collapse onto one person or disappear entirely.

People are more willing to contribute when the system is understandable, their roles are clear, and joining doesn't mean inheriting every unresolved problem.

Over time, that kind of participation creates more capacity for participation.

But perhaps the deeper test of culture comes when people disagree.

I've been thinking about a related principle: a principle has to survive disagreement.

Organizations face a parallel challenge.

**For a principle to survive disagreement repeatedly, the culture itself has to be capable of carrying disagreement.**

Without hiding from it. Without automatically escalating it. Without outsourcing the responsibility to someone else. Without reducing the people involved to the problem.

That requires trust, transparency, clear ownership, and enough room for people to remain in relationship even when they don't share the same interpretation.

Looking back, I no longer believe the question was whether professional management was good or bad.

The better question was what we were asking management to solve.

If the problem was administration, expertise, or workload, outside support could be exactly what we needed.

But if the problem was unclear ownership, invisible reasoning, weak relationships, or an inability to work through disagreement, another intermediary wasn't necessarily going to repair it.

We could outsource work. We could purchase expertise. We could use technology to expand what people were capable of doing.

But we still had to decide how we were going to participate with one another.

Perhaps that's what healthy governance ultimately tries to create.

Not a system in which conflict disappears.

**A system resilient enough to metabolize conflict without losing itself.**
