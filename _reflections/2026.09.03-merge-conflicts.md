## Culture as an Open-Source Project

I've been thinking about why people have such a hard time discussing pronouns, religion, politics, gender, morality, and other subjects where everyone seems certain that the other side just doesn't get it.

A metaphor that's helping me is GitHub.

Imagine culture as an enormous open-source project that nobody consciously designed.

There isn't one repository. There are thousands of overlapping repositories: religions, political traditions, national cultures, families, workplaces, communities, relationships.

And every individual is running their own fork.

We inherited much of our code before we could even speak. Parents, schools, communities, and experiences contributed. Along the way, we made modifications, borrowed from other branches, and developed our own interpretations.

Most of this happens without our awareness.

Then two people meet and say:

*This is what that word means.*

But sometimes they've encountered a merge conflict.

## When Branches Collide

Take pronouns.

One cultural branch might understand pronouns primarily as grammatical references to biological sex.

Another might understand pronouns as participating in social identity, where how someone wishes to be recognized matters.

From inside either framework, its interpretation can seem obvious.

So one person hears, "My pronouns are they/them," and experiences a category error.

Another hears someone deliberately refusing those pronouns and experiences a relational or moral violation.

They appear to be arguing about a small piece of language.

Underneath it may be different understandings of identity, authority, relationship, biology, language, and reality.

Recognizing those frameworks doesn't settle the disagreement or erase the consequences of how people treat one another.

But it may help us understand why something that seems obvious to one person feels fundamentally different to another.

## Going Up a Level

The same thing happens with religion.

Someone can speak beautifully about what Jesus means to them. But it's harder to ask:

*What is Jesus for you? What does that understanding organize in your life? What assumptions does it contain? What would feel threatened if you understood it differently?*

Those questions move us up a level.

Instead of only asking whether a belief is right or wrong, we begin examining the system through which we're making meaning.

And that can be frightening.

Because these systems aren't merely collections of ideas.

They contain belonging. Family. Morality. Identity. Safety. Sometimes God.

To see my worldview as one evolving branch among many can feel like I'm betraying it.

But recognizing that I inhabit a framework doesn't require abandoning what I believe.

It means becoming capable of examining the framework through which I believe it.

## Everyone's Blind Spot

This isn't particularly a conservative, progressive, religious, or secular problem.

A progressive person may easily recognize Christianity as a cultural framework while struggling to see their own moral vocabulary as one.

A conservative may recognize ideology everywhere except within conservatism.

A Christian may recognize other religions as interpretive traditions while experiencing their own as simply reality.

And those of us who enjoy thinking at the meta-level have exactly the same trap.

We can become attached to being the people who see the repositories.

Then we've created another identity.

Another tribe.

Another branch convinced that everyone else needs to merge into ours.

## Three Kinds of Harmony

I don't think the goal is to eliminate our local identities or arrive at one universal interpretation.

Perhaps there are different kinds of harmony.

**Local harmony:** We agree about what is true.

**Pluralistic harmony:** We don't agree, but we allow one another to exist.

**Meta-harmony:** We become curious about how our different systems generate meaning, what each is protecting, and whether we can create relationship without requiring one system to conquer the other.

But even meta-harmony can't become compulsory.

If I tell someone, "You need to transcend your worldview and recognize that it's only a framework," I've simply turned transcendence into another doctrine.

And seeing different frameworks doesn't mean treating every claim as equally true or every action as harmless.

Some disagreements involve real consequences. Some require boundaries. Some may never be reconciled.

The value of meta-awareness isn't that it resolves every conflict.

It's that we can become more conscious of what we're actually disagreeing about.

Perhaps the harder practice is being able to hold two things at once:

*This is the system through which I currently understand reality. I may believe it deeply.*

*And I can still become curious about the system through which you understand reality.*

## Under Pressure

That's relatively easy when nothing important is happening.

It becomes harder when my children are at stake. My marriage. My dignity. My community. My politics. My identity. My God.

Under pressure, our minds have powerful reasons to collapse complexity.

*This is right. That is wrong. This is us. That is them. Defend this.*

At precisely the moment when understanding another perspective might matter most, our own framework can become almost impossible to question.

Which makes me wonder whether one of the most important capacities we could develop is something surprisingly simple:

**Can I remain aware of the framework I'm standing inside, even when that framework is telling me I can't afford to question it?**

## Seeing the Branch We're Standing On

Perhaps that's where consciousness becomes a practice rather than merely an idea.

Not finding the perfect repository.

Not getting everybody onto the same branch.

Not abandoning our convictions simply because we recognize how they were formed.

But learning to see the branch we're standing on—even during the merge conflict.
