It is relatively easy to believe in dignity when the person asking for it is someone we understand.

It is easy to believe in fairness when we feel unfairly treated.

It is easy to believe that context matters when our own behavior is being interpreted without the context we were living through.

The harder question comes when the positions change.

When I believe the other person is wrong.

When their behavior frustrates me.

When I have authority they do not.

When I could escalate.

When understanding them feels like it might somehow weaken my own position.

That may be where a principle becomes something more than an idea.

It has to survive the disagreement.

## The Easy Version of Principle

Most of us have moral principles we believe organize how we live.

People should be treated with dignity.

We should listen before judging.

Power should be exercised responsibly.

Context matters.

People deserve an opportunity to be understood.

These ideas sound straightforward in the abstract.

But abstraction removes almost everything that makes practicing them difficult.

There is no history in the abstract. No fear. No accumulated frustration. No competing account of what happened. No institution telling us what we are technically allowed to do. No person across the table whom we have already decided should know better.

The principle becomes harder to recognize once it enters an actual relationship.

And sometimes that difficulty reveals something uncomfortable:

We may not have been committed to the principle itself.

We may have been committed to the principle when it supported our position.

## Dignity Is Not Agreement

One reason this becomes difficult is that we often confuse understanding someone with agreeing with them.

They are not the same thing.

To take another person's experience seriously does not require us to adopt their interpretation. To treat someone with dignity does not require us to conclude that their behavior was appropriate.

Compassion does not require closeness. Understanding does not eliminate accountability. Listening does not mean surrendering our own experience.

There is another possibility.

I can believe that my perspective matters and still make room for yours. I can believe that something needs to change and still care about what happens to you in the process. I can choose greater distance without requiring you to become less human in my mind.

Sometimes the healthiest response to a difficult relationship is not greater agreement or greater intimacy.

It is a different distance.

Enough distance to recover our center. Enough distance to see the field more clearly. Enough distance to respond rather than react.

Distance does not have to mean rejection. It can be one of the ways we remain capable of relationship without abandoning ourselves inside it.

## Finding Our Center of Gravity

Aikido has given me a physical language for something I increasingly notice in relationships.

When we lose our center, force begins making decisions for us.

We lean too far into the other person. We pull away abruptly. We brace. We push. We become preoccupied with controlling their movement because we can no longer feel our own.

The same thing happens emotionally.

Someone says something that threatens an identity, a relationship, our sense of fairness, or our understanding of what happened.

Our attention moves outward. Toward them. What they did. What they should understand. What they need to change. What they are refusing to see.

And somewhere in that movement, we can lose our own center of gravity.

Recovering it does not resolve the disagreement.

It changes our relationship to the disagreement.

I can still be angry. Still believe something was wrong. Still want accountability. Still choose distance.

But those choices no longer have to emerge entirely from the force coming toward me.

I have somewhere else to stand.

## Power and the Ability to Escalate

Power introduces another complication.

When we are affected by someone else's authority, we tend to experience its consequences. When we possess authority, we tend to experience our intentions.

I may know that I am protecting a community. Enforcing an agreement. Creating necessary distance. Responding to behavior that concerns me.

Those intentions may be completely sincere.

But the person on the receiving end does not experience my intentions directly.

They experience what I do.

This does not mean authority is wrong. It means authority creates a responsibility to see more than our own reasons for using it.

The question is not only:

**Can I justify what I am doing?**

There is another question:

**What is the force I am adding entering into?**

Someone may already be frightened. Overwhelmed. Isolated. Defensive. Ashamed. Angry. Carrying pressures I cannot see.

That does not mean I abandon judgment.

It means judgment is no longer the only capacity available to me.

One of the most consequential forms of power in conflict is the ability to escalate.

We can send the email. Invoke the rule. Call the lawyer. Expose the inconsistency. Apply pressure. Demand an answer. Prove the point.

Sometimes escalation is necessary.

But the fact that we possess the ability does not tell us whether using it is wise.

That distinction may be one of the clearest places where agency appears.

Survival tends to collapse the field.

Fight. Defend. Prove. Escape. Make them understand.

Under enough pressure, the existence of five possible responses can feel like the existence of only one.

Agency reopens the field.

Not by pretending that nothing important is happening. Not by suppressing anger or abandoning conviction. But by returning to center long enough to ask:

**What am I actually trying to create here?**

Victory? Protection? Repair? Accountability? Understanding? More distance? A future relationship? A clean ending?

Those goals do not always require the same response.

Sometimes strength means moving toward. Sometimes it means stepping away. Sometimes it means changing the distance without changing the principle.

## Distance and Expression

The amount of distance a relationship can sustain is not necessarily a measure of how much we care.

Sometimes closeness supports humanity. Sometimes closeness overwhelms our capacity to extend it.

The question becomes less:

**How close should we be?**

And more:

**At what distance can I remain centered enough to meet this person as a human being?**

That distance may change.

Relationships move. People change. Capacity changes.

What required separation at one moment may later allow greater proximity. What once felt easy may later require more space.

The principle remains.

The distance adapts.

And if a principle has to survive disagreement, that does not mean it should always produce identical behavior.

Dignity may require listening in one situation. Greater distance in another. Consequences somewhere else. Patience here. Directness there. Restraint now. Intervention later.

The principle can remain stable while its expression changes.

That may be one of the difficulties of mature leadership.

Rules are easier. Rules tell us what to do.

Principles require us to remain present enough to perceive what the situation is asking from us.

And because that discernment is imperfect, we will sometimes get it wrong.

## The Things We Learn About Ourselves

Conflict has an uncomfortable way of revealing patterns we did not previously understand.

Sometimes recovering our center also changes what we are able to see about our own participation in the conflict.

We may discover that what felt from the inside like urgency, persistence, or an attempt to restore connection was experienced by someone else as pressure.

We may see that our response to distance created more distance, or that our effort to resolve an activated situation contributed to keeping it activated.

That recognition does not require us to abandon our account of what happened.

It asks us to make our account large enough to include our own effect on the field.

Recognizing those patterns can be painful.

But there are two mistakes available to us.

One is refusing to see them. The other is believing that seeing them requires us to condemn everything about ourselves that came before.

Neither seems especially useful.

We can understand where a pattern came from without using that understanding to avoid responsibility. We can acknowledge that our behavior affected other people without deciding that everything we intended was false. We can recognize the good we were trying to do and still become more capable of doing it differently.

That is not hypocrisy.

It is learning.

Perhaps transformation is less about becoming the person who never gets something wrong and more about becoming increasingly capable of recognizing when we have lost our center, finding it again, and choosing differently from there.

## What We Teach When We Disagree

I think about this increasingly as a parent.

Children learn from what we tell them. But they may learn even more from watching what remains available to us when life becomes difficult.

Do our principles disappear when someone angers us? Does disagreement turn people into enemies? Does having power mean we have to use all of it? Can we acknowledge where we contributed to a problem without collapsing into shame? Can we protect someone we love without needing to dehumanize someone else? Can we create distance without turning distance into punishment? Can we remain capable of curiosity while still having convictions?

Those questions teach something larger than conflict resolution.

They teach what power is for. They teach what accountability can look like. They teach whether distance and care can coexist. They teach whether strength and compassion are opposites.

And they teach whether our moral principles are descriptions of the people we hope to be, or practices we are willing to return to when being that person becomes difficult.

## The Principle Has to Survive the Disagreement

Perhaps that is the test.

Not whether I can articulate the principle. Not whether I recognize it when someone fails to extend it to me. Not even whether I have practiced it successfully before.

The harder test is whether I can still see it from inside the disagreement.

Whether dignity remains dignity when I believe the other person is wrong. Whether humanity remains visible when I am angry. Whether restraint remains available when I possess force. Whether I can change the distance without abandoning the relationship to the other person's humanity. Whether curiosity remains possible when certainty feels safer. Whether accountability can include me without requiring me to become my worst moment.

A principle that exists only when it is convenient may be a preference.

A principle that applies only to people we understand may be an affiliation.

A principle that protects only our side may be a strategy.

But if it is something we truly believe about how human beings should treat one another, then sooner or later it will have to cross the disagreement.

Perhaps that does not require us to stand close.

Perhaps it requires something harder:

To find our center. To choose the distance consciously. And from there, to see whether the principle is still alive.
