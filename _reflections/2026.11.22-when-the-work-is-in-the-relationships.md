**We organize work around functions. But change flows through relationships.**

Organizations are remarkably good at defining functions.

We establish roles, responsibilities, processes, reporting structures, and interfaces so people can coordinate their work.

And these structures matter.

And also true is that there's another dimension of organizational life that doesn't fit neatly into those structures: the relationships through which people develop understanding, influence one another, and discover what's possible together.

A team can have a clear plan, assigned responsibilities, and regular meetings, yet still struggle to move together. Everyone may be performing their function while carrying different interpretations of what matters, what was decided, or what others need.

The work is organized. But something isn't connecting.

## Beyond Social Fluency

This is where I see an important distinction between social skill and relational capability.

Someone can be socially fluent. They can communicate effectively, participate in meetings, collaborate with colleagues, and navigate organizational structures.

Relational capability builds on that foundation and goes further.

It's not simply about being good with people or building a professional network.

It's about recognizing relationships themselves as a medium through which change happens.

Cultivating trust. Connecting people across boundaries. Inviting perspectives that aren't yet represented. Recognizing patterns in how people interact, interpret situations, and coordinate action.

And sometimes, noticing that what appears to be a process problem or a technical impediment is actually a breakdown in shared understanding.

## Relationships in Technical Work

This relational dimension isn't limited to meetings or face-to-face conversations.

Consider a change introduced through a pull request in a GitHub repository.

The code may be sound. Automated checks may pass. The proposed change may even solve a genuine problem.

But whether that change is understood, accepted, and adopted can depend on more than its technical merits.

Who needs to understand why the change matters? What concerns might emerge? Which perspectives haven't been considered? How might the proposal affect people working elsewhere in the system?

The pull request itself can become a place where relational capability is exercised, even asynchronously.

The way we introduce an idea, invite participation, respond to concerns, and build shared understanding can influence [what becomes possible](/reflections/when-the-idea-dies-on-the-vine/).

And as AI agents increasingly participate in technical work, this dimension of change becomes worth paying closer attention to.

AI can help generate solutions, propose changes, and perform more of the functional work. But the human context in which that work takes place includes relationships, shared histories, expectations, and cultural patterns that shape how change unfolds.

The challenge isn't simply producing more changes. It's understanding how those changes enter a living system of people and relationships.

Technical execution and relational capability aren't separate worlds. They often meet in the same work.

## Two Ways of Engaging With Work

This isn't a distinction between two kinds of people.

It's a distinction between two ways of engaging with the work.

We can approach a situation functionally, asking what needs to be done, who owns it, and which process to follow.

Or we can also approach it relationally, asking who needs to be connected, what perspectives are missing, and what might become possible through greater shared understanding.

Both matter.

But when we're navigating complexity, especially in the [human layer of an organization](/reflections/what-i-mean-by-human-layer-engineering/), functional coordination alone may not be enough.

Sometimes the most important work isn't completing another task or introducing another structure.

It's cultivating the relationships through which people can begin to move together.

**Perhaps we need to pay as much attention to the relationships that make work possible as we do to the structures that organize it.**
