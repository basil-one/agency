We've probably all experienced entering a community and struggling to find our place.

We spend energy trying to understand unwritten expectations, whose perspectives carry influence, and what's safe to question.

Perhaps we've also experienced something quite different.

A community where we begin to understand the spirit of what brings people together. Where we can find our footing without having to decipher every relationship or become like everyone else.

What makes that experience possible?

## Something to Return To

Rules and procedures can be useful when we encounter familiar situations. Principles become particularly valuable when we encounter ambiguity and circumstances for which there isn't a prescribed response.

[Pattern thinking](https://agency.basil.one/reflections/when-do-patterns-matter/) helps us make sense of what's happening. Principled grounding helps us discern how to engage with what's happening.

When a community makes its principles accessible and reasonably clear without becoming overly prescriptive, newcomers have somewhere to begin and existing members have something to return to.

This doesn't eliminate conflict, nor should it. Conflict can generate understanding, deepen relationships, and open new possibilities.

Shared principles give us common ground from which to navigate our differences.

**We can find common ground without becoming alike.**

## What Principles Ask of Us

It's relatively easy to articulate principles. Something more is required when [the practice is tested](https://agency.basil.one/reflections/when-the-practice-is-tested/).

We may need to loosen our attachment to a preference, an interpretation, or a narrative we've constructed about ourselves and others.

This becomes particularly consequential for those who hold influence through authority, rank, or informal status.

The greater our influence, the more our willingness to be guided by shared principles shapes the conditions in which others participate.

We may need to reconsider a decision or relinquish some control.

It's one thing to establish principles that guide others. It's another to remain accountable to them when we have the power to do otherwise.

[A principle has to survive the disagreement](https://agency.basil.one/reflections/principle-survives-disagreement/).

When it doesn't, people learn to orient around personalities. They expend energy anticipating reactions, navigating preferences, and determining when it's safe to disagree.

Fear can gradually become a more influential organizing force than shared intention.

## What Grows From the Investment

There is real human labor involved in cultivating shared principles and continually integrating them through experience.

But something develops when enough people are willing to make that investment.

Principles that hold up under pressure become increasingly reliable. Our understanding deepens. Their expression may become simpler as our capacity to work with them grows.

They become durable grounding for reconciling intentions and generative sources of energy for exploring new possibilities.

People can begin to trust something more enduring than the disposition of those who hold influence.

And perhaps more of our collective energy becomes available for cultivating what brought us together.

**Principles have a cost. So does living without shared grounding.**

What are we willing to invest in?
