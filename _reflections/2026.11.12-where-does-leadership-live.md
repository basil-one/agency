When we think of leadership, we often imagine someone at the center—providing direction, making decisions, keeping everyone aligned.

But some of the best leadership I've experienced hasn't looked like that. A group can stall because nobody decides. It can also stall because someone decides before others have had a chance to contribute.

Think of musicians playing together. An orchestra may have a conductor. At a jam session, people find their way without one. In a jazz ensemble, the lead may shift from player to player.

All can make remarkable music. What changes is how they coordinate.

## Three Ways Order Emerges

I've been reflecting on three ways that happens.

**Hierarchy** organizes through authority. Someone sets direction and coordinates the parts—useful when clarity and decisive action matter.

**Anarchy** relies on autonomy and voluntary participation. People contribute without a central authority directing them.

**Heterarchy** works through relationships and mutual influence. One person offers an idea, another responds, and leadership shifts as the situation unfolds.

These aren't rigid categories. An orchestra still requires listening; jazz still needs [structure](/reflections/culture-is-infrastructure/). The question isn't which form is best, but what the moment calls for.

## The Energy We Bring

The same question applies to our energy.

In [Circle3: Three Guiding Moves for Leading Difficult Change](https://circle.basil.one/paper), a lightweight approach to helping groups move through stuck change, I use an [Energy Awareness Reflection](https://circle.basil.one/lenses/energy/) as one of its Leadership Lenses. It helps us notice how energy shifts across conversations and emotional contexts.

**Driver energy** brings direction and closure. **Organizer energy** establishes structure. **Collaborator energy** connects people and perspectives. **Visionary energy** opens possibilities.

These aren't types of people. They're different energies we can access and cultivate. Each can help, or overwhelm the room when it doesn't fit.

I recognize them all in myself. I enjoy exploring possibilities and integrating perspectives. But when ambiguity drags on without insight, or loose ends remain unresolved, I want to make a decision and move.

Sometimes that impulse helps. Sometimes it risks cutting off something that needs more room.

Like musicians, we need to sense when to explore, establish a rhythm, or bring a phrase to resolution.

## Coherence Without Control

I've come to see leadership less as a style and more as an [adaptive practice](/reflections/we-walk-together/).

Sometimes someone must decide. Sometimes the group needs space to experiment. Sometimes the most useful move is simply to help people hear one another.

That brings me to a distinction between alignment and coherence.

Alignment can mean everyone following the same score. Coherence is more like musicians improvising: each plays a different part, yet they listen and respond well enough for the music to hold together.

Their differences aren't obstacles. They're part of what makes the music possible.

**Leadership cultivates coherence among [autonomous, interconnected agents](/reflections/what-i-mean-by-human-layer-engineering/).**

This doesn't mean avoiding authority, decisions, or structure. It means using them without making everyone dependent on the person at the center. The aim isn't [control or consensus](/reflections/principle-survives-disagreement/), but coherent action that preserves agency.

Perhaps leadership lives in recognizing when to step forward, when to step aside, and when to let something emerge between us.
