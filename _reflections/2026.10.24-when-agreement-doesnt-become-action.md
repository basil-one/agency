Imagine you've been working with a few colleagues on improving something in your organization. You've experienced the problem, explored alternatives, and developed a proposal you believe could make things better.

You share it with others. The conversations seem encouraging. People recognize the problem. Some agree that your proposal makes sense.

And then, not much happens.

Another priority takes precedence. A decision gets deferred. The people you need are busy with something else. What initially felt promising gradually loses momentum.

Nobody necessarily rejected the idea. It just stopped moving.

Perhaps you've experienced this. I certainly have.

It's tempting to conclude that we need to communicate better, find stronger sponsorship, or push harder. Sometimes we do. But I wonder if [something more fundamental](/reflections/what-i-mean-by-human-layer-engineering/) is happening.

**Understanding a problem isn't the same as feeling that it's worth solving. And agreeing with a solution isn't the same as being willing to invest in it.**

## Making Room for Change

The present has a powerful advantage. People know how to operate within it. They've developed relationships, routines, and ways of navigating its constraints. Their attention is already committed.

For something new to take hold, something else has to make room.

You, me, enough of us—we need to become willing and able to say no, or at least *not now*, to something we're already doing. Otherwise, even a compelling idea can remain stuck in idea land.

That's a real cost, particularly when today's priorities already feel overwhelming.

So perhaps our challenge isn't simply to gain acceptance for a proposal. It's to develop an understanding of the problem, and an alternative future, that enough people consider worth making room for.

But how do we get there?

## Bringing Energy to the Effort

I don't think we can assume that the right conversations will happen on their own.

We'll need to seek people out, listen to what matters to them, bring small groups together, and return to conversations that haven't yet produced shared understanding.

This requires different expressions of energy. Sometimes we're driving movement, sometimes organizing, collaborating, or helping people imagine what could become possible. These aren't fixed roles or labels for people. They're ways we can contribute as the situation changes.

Mary Lynn Manns and Linda Rising captured many of these recurring challenges and useful responses in [Fearless Change](https://fearlesschangepatterns.com/). Their patterns aren't rigid instructions. They describe situations people repeatedly encounter, along with approaches that have helped others navigate them. We can draw on these patterns as we engage people, build relationships, and develop momentum.

But there's an important distinction between bringing energy to an effort and pushing people toward our preferred answer.

Someone who appears resistant may be seeing something we haven't recognized. Their priorities may be reasonable. Our proposal may be incomplete.

And perhaps we're not yet solving the right problem.

## Making Our Thinking Visible

Eliyahu Goldratt's systems thinking offers a useful challenge. The difficulties we're experiencing may be symptoms of a deeper **core problem**, sustained by a **core conflict** between competing needs.

Beneath that conflict may be assumptions that make the situation appear unavoidable. Some are valid. Others may be incomplete or erroneous.

If we don't examine them, we risk spending considerable energy solving the wrong problem.

Christopher Alexander's work offers a way to make this thinking visible through patterns: recurring problems, the forces shaping them, and responses that can be adapted to different circumstances.

I've been finding it useful to combine that structure with Goldratt's inquiry into underlying problems, conflicts, and assumptions:

- **Context:** What situation are we in?
- **Problem:** What difficulty are we trying to address?
- **Forces:** What needs, tensions, conflicts, and assumptions shape it?
- **Solution:** What might work?
- **Resulting Context:** What changes if we try it?

Initially, our understanding may be messy. People bring different experiences, frustrations, interpretations, and ideas. We may need to unpack those perspectives before we're ready to reconcile them.

We can capture these conversations and use AI to help identify themes, surface assumptions, and develop a working representation of what we're learning.

Then we bring it back to the people involved.

Does this reflect what we're experiencing? What's missing? What might we have misunderstood?

Through repeated reflection, criticism, and integration, we can develop a clearer understanding of the situation and what we might do about it.

Not necessarily agreement about everything, but enough coherence to make meaningful choices together.

## Walking Together Through Change

I've encountered versions of this problem many times over the years, in different organizations and under different circumstances. The details change, but some of the difficulties are remarkably familiar.

People struggle to maintain shared attention, different interpretations pull conversations apart, and promising decisions don't always become lasting commitments.

Over time, I've come to describe three recurring responses as [Circle3](https://circle.basil.one/paper), a small pattern language for leading difficult change.

**Establish the Circle** helps us bring people into a recurring space where understanding can develop. **Integrate the Conversation** helps us work with differing perspectives and tensions. **Reconcile the Intention** helps us make our emerging shared understanding visible enough to guide decisions and commitments.

These are guiding moves rather than sequential steps. We can return to them as circumstances change.

Together with Fearless Change and structured systems thinking, these practices help us navigate an effort that may take several weeks of focused attention, perhaps longer.

We'll need individual conversations, group sessions, reflection, experimentation, and follow-through. We'll encounter competing priorities and disagreements. And we'll need to keep examining whether our understanding reflects what's actually happening.

Our working representation becomes both a map of where we believe we are and a compass pointing toward the future we're trying to create. We can return to it, challenge it, and let it evolve.

We don't need everyone to think alike, or a perfect map before beginning. We need enough shared understanding to move, enough curiosity to recognize when we're wrong, and enough commitment to keep learning together.

Along the way, we may discover that the alternative future looks different from the one we initially imagined.

That isn't necessarily failure. It may mean we're [developing a better understanding of the system we're trying to change](/reflections/dont-fear-the-reorganization/).

And perhaps that's the deeper opportunity.

Not simply gaining acceptance for a proposal, but developing the collective capacity to understand our circumstances, make room for what matters, and participate in shaping something better.

**Change is hard. Let's shape it together.**
