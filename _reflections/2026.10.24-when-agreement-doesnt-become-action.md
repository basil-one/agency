Imagine you've been working with a few colleagues on improving something in your organization. You've experienced the problem, explored alternatives, and developed a proposal you believe could make things better.

You share it with others. The conversations seem encouraging. People recognize the problem. Some agree that your proposal makes sense.

And then, not much happens.

Another priority takes precedence. A decision gets deferred. What initially felt promising gradually loses momentum.

Nobody necessarily rejected the idea. It just stopped moving.

Perhaps you've experienced this. I certainly have.

It's tempting to conclude that we need to communicate better, find stronger sponsorship, or push harder. Sometimes we do. But I wonder if [something more fundamental](/reflections/what-i-mean-by-human-layer-engineering/) is happening.

**Understanding a problem isn't the same as feeling that it's worth solving. And agreeing with a solution isn't the same as being willing to invest in it.**

## Making Room for Change

The present has a powerful advantage. People know how to operate within it. They've developed relationships, routines, and ways of navigating its constraints. Their attention is already committed.

For something new to take hold, something else has to make room.

You, me, enough of us—we need to become willing and able to say no, or at least *not now*, to something we're already doing. Otherwise, even a compelling idea can remain stuck in idea land.

That's a real cost, particularly when today's priorities already feel overwhelming.

So perhaps our challenge isn't simply gaining acceptance for a proposal. It's helping enough people recognize a future worth making room for.

## Bringing Energy to the Effort

That won't happen on its own. We'll need to seek people out, listen to what matters to them, bring small groups together, and return to conversations that haven't yet produced shared understanding.

Different moments call for different energy—driving movement, organizing, collaborating, or helping others imagine what could be possible. These aren't fixed labels for people, but ways we can contribute as circumstances change.

Mary Lynn Manns and Linda Rising captured many recurring challenges of introducing new ideas in [Fearless Change](https://fearlesschangepatterns.com/). Their patterns offer practical responses drawn from experience: *Personal Touch* encourages individual conversations, *Champion Skeptic* invites influential critics to help improve an idea, and *Trial Run* creates an opportunity to learn through a small experiment.

But bringing energy to change isn't the same as pushing people toward our answer.

Someone who appears resistant may see something we've missed. Their priorities may be reasonable. And perhaps we're not yet solving the right problem.

## Making Our Thinking Visible

Eliyahu Goldratt's systems thinking offers a useful challenge. What we're experiencing may be symptoms of a deeper **core problem**, sustained by a **core conflict** between competing needs. Beneath that conflict may be assumptions that make it appear unavoidable—some valid, others incomplete or erroneous.

If we don't examine them, we risk solving the wrong problem.

Christopher Alexander's pattern thinking offers a complementary way to structure and share our understanding:

- **Context:** What situation are we in?
- **Problem:** What difficulty are we trying to address?
- **Forces:** What needs, tensions, conflicts, and assumptions shape it?
- **Solution:** What might work?
- **Resulting Context:** What happens when we apply the solution?

Initially, our understanding may be messy. People bring different experiences, frustrations, and interpretations. We can unpack those perspectives through conversation, then use AI to help identify themes, surface assumptions, and develop a working representation.

But the representation isn't the group's understanding until people have had a chance to reason with it.

Does it reflect what we're experiencing? What's missing? What have we misunderstood?

Through repeated reflection, criticism, and integration, we can develop something people recognize themselves in. Not necessarily agreement about everything, but enough shared understanding to make meaningful choices together.

## From Understanding to Commitment

I've encountered versions of this problem many times over the years. The circumstances change, but the difficulties are familiar: attention fragments, conversations pull in different directions, and promising decisions don't become lasting commitments.

Through these experiences, I've come to recognize three recurring moves that help groups navigate these challenges. I've named them [Circle3: Three Guiding Moves for Leading Difficult Change](https://circle.basil.one/paper).

**Establish the Circle** brings together people willing to sustain attention. **Integrate the Conversation** helps us work through differing perspectives and tensions. **Reconcile the Intention** makes our emerging shared understanding visible enough to guide decisions and commitments.

These are guiding moves, not rigid sequential steps. Together with Fearless Change and structured systems thinking, they give us practices to draw upon as we navigate the journey.

That journey may require several weeks of focused attention, difficult conversations, experimentation, and follow-through. Our understanding will evolve, and the future worth pursuing may look different from the one we originally imagined.

But perhaps that's the point.

**The goal isn't simply to get people to agree with an idea. It's to develop enough shared understanding, willingness, and commitment that something actually changes.**

Not everyone needs to think alike. Not everyone needs to contribute in the same way. But enough of us need to make room, participate, and carry what we're learning into practice.

**Change is hard. Let's shape it together.**
